### 1. MEGA-DEATH
(from the Notes block just before the chapter heading)

**Players:** Whole ensemble, unlimited
**Setup:** Empty stage
**Goal:** Get improvisers used to killing each other, played with elation
**Rules:** 1. Enter and kill whoever is onstage within five to ten seconds or less. 2. Repeat until the stage is filled with bodies.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Can be hysterically funny when played with elation

---

### 2. WORD-AT-A-TIME

**Players:** 2 volunteers plus a leader (original demonstration form)
**Setup:** Volunteers sit beside the leader
**Goal:** Prevent anyone from thinking past the next word. Sentences must be grammatical and make sense
**Rules:**
1. Leader opens: "Let's invent a story by adding a word each: Sally..."
2. Each player contributes one word in turn: "Was..." / "Going..." / "Mad..." (leader stirs things up here) / "Because..." / "Her..." / "Father..." / "Wanted..." / "To..." / "Put..." / "His..." / "Horse..." / "Into..." / "Her..." / "Stable..."
3. Continue until the story fizzles or completes
4. If you start "Henry..." planning "Henry was late for school", the next player may plan "Henry took a bath", the next "Henry took Betty to lunch", so by your turn Henry's lateness is gone forever
**Variations:** See Word-at-a-Time (in a Circle) below
**Coaching:** Some stories fizzle after one sentence, a few complete themselves, unusual early on

---

### 3. WORD-AT-A-TIME (IN A CIRCLE)

**Players:** 6 to 8 in a circle
**Setup:** Circle formation
**Goal:** Fast, grammatical, meaningful word contributions
**Rules:**
1. Group invents "stories" by adding a word each around the circle
2. Anyone who hesitates, is ungrammatical, or makes no sense is ejected
3. Ejectees become the Judges who say "Speed up!" and enforce the rules
**Variations:** 1. Group claps rhythmically, words synchronized to the beat. 2. Each player says a word then points to someone at random who must add the next word. 3. Letters, then stories, written by groups of preferably four students
**Coaching:** Judges police pace and correctness

---

### 4. PERFORMANCE WORD-AT-A-TIME

**Players:** 2
**Setup:** Players stay close together, acting out the story as they invent it
**Goal:** Physicalize a Word-at-a-Time story without degenerating into gossip
**Rules:**
1. Players narrate and act simultaneously: "We are walking along the beach..."
2. Beginners are prompted with physical commands: "Walk forwards!" / "When we see a cave..." / "Go in!" / "We enter the cave and explore it..." / "Have flashlights!" / "Taking our flashlights we go along a dark passage..." / "Hear something." / "We hear someone singing..." / "See the person."
3. Once they have the hang of it, stop prompting and let them develop it alone
**Variations:** 1. If prompting stalls, ask players to add the word "suddenly". 2. One player plays with closed eyes. 3. Both close eyes while the class protects them: bird sounds in a meadow, tugging ankles for little people, flicked water drops plus wind hiss plus flapped coats for a storm
**Coaching:** Be protective. If the story takes over, players forget where they are, grab them before they sprint into brick walls. Excellent challenge, unlike most games. The Three Canadians of Loose Moose play it as street theatre. Closed eyes yield vivid inner reality

### 5. AVOIDING THE WOLF

**Players:** Any storytellers
**Setup:** Storytelling improvisation, solo or paired
**Goal:** Stop beginners dodging encounters with the "wolf", meaning anything frightening
**Rules:**
1. Hero must interact with the wolf
2. Failure patterns: buying bread and going home ("the most unwolflike thing they could imagine"), meeting a gigantic walking suit of armour and fleeing ("We run out side and go home!")
3. Success pattern: "The monster paralysed us with its sting, and wrapped us in a cocoon of threads and began dragging us towards its lair..."
4. Teaching sequence: first ask students to meet a monster and escape from it
5. Then dare them to interact and either destroy it or be destroyed. If destroyed, continue in heaven or hell or inside the monster
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Tame it, ride it, make love to it, or be torn to pieces, but interact. The Senoi Indians, dream controllers, give the same advice. Apparent talent is willingness to accept the hero role

---

### 6. NO ADJECTIVES OR BUTS

**Players:** Any Word-at-a-Time group
**Setup:** Applied once players are fluent
**Goal:** Stop wimping by delaying the noun
**Rules:**
1. Spot the wimp pattern: "Cliff took Betty into his long, big, grey, enormous, vibrating, picturesque..."
2. Forbid adjectives, or forbid "buts"
3. Ban cliches such as finding treasure
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Only impose when fluency exists

---

### 7. WHAT COMES NEXT?

**Players:** 1 hero onstage, class or audience suggests
**Setup:** Single player centre stage. Instruction: "Make no decisions for yourself. Just ask, 'What comes next?' and then do what we tell you. If the scene is boring, blame us."
**Goal:** Expose the struggle between positive and negative suggestion, train acceptance, reveal defensive storytelling habits
**Rules:**
1. Hero asks "What comes next?" and performs each answer literally
2. Early suggestions fool the improviser: "The phone rings", "You jump up and down!", "You play the trombone", "You squeeze a zit"
3. Coach interrupts: "Stop sidetracking. Allow one action to lead to another!"
4. Analysis moment: "How did the scene begin?" / "The phone rang." / "Well, the knock at the door is a rejection of the phone." / "So what do we do?" / "Answer the phone!"
5. Name the blocks as they appear. "The phone's stuck to its cradle!" is the genius of being dull: original, block, wimp, bridging, instant trouble, cancels action, negative, gag. "You have a conversation" wimps because no responsibility is taken for what's said. "A man bursts in the door with a gun!" is sidetracking and being original
6. Force positivity: mother-in-law suggestion is meant negatively, so demand positive next steps: "She's coming to see you", "Make yourself respectable!", "You go to the cupboard", "Take out a rifle" (negative-looking but not story killing), "You load it"
7. Enforce promises: loading the rifle promises a shooting. Students bridge wildly: "You shoot yourself in the foot!" / "No he doesn't!" / "The bullets are the wrong size!" / "No they aren't!" Coach: "Ninety-nine per cent of the audience expect him to shoot his mother-in-law. So shoot her. Stop trying to be creative!"
8. Sidetracks get vetoed: salesman at the door, alarm clock rings. "Just shoot her!" / "Bang! What comes next?" / "She shoots back!" Everyone laughs, story advances without cancelling the mother-in-law
9. Negativity diagnosis, Lingen example: "You stand up" / "You break your leg" / "You try to stand up again and your other leg breaks" / "You break your arm", stopped before teeth snap off on a medicine box. Verdict: "The story can't work because it's in a loop, and there's no platform, and it's insanely negative."
**Variations:**
1. Two-person Version: men control a man, women control a woman, someone shouts for the wrong sex, funnier if warned against. Try sibling rivalries, parent-child scenes, strangers meeting scenes
2. Fifty purely positive suggestions in a row, feel the wave of negativity mounting
3. Alternate positive with negative: "Tom falls out of the window" / "a beautiful nurse takes him home to bandage him" / "her boyfriend is enraged" / "Tom is a better fighter"
4. Heroes add some of their own ideas to the audience's (not for beginners)
5. Hero also asks "What do I feel?" or "What's ahead of me?" or "What's that over there?"
6. A presenter questions the audience about the hero: "What emotion does he have?", "Why does she want revenge?", "What secret is he hiding from her?"
**Coaching:** Return to this game like body-builders to weights. Opening suggestions like stuck-to-the-chair, run-around-the-chair, stand-on-the-chair are refusals to move the hero and go unjustified. Without analysis of these defensive strategies the game teaches relatively little

### 8. DON'T SPIN YOUR WHEELS

**Players:** Small groups (class divided)
**Setup:** Groups play What Comes Next unsupervised
**Goal:** Kill dead stories instantly instead of grinding on
**Rules:**
1. Play What Comes Next in small groups
2. The moment the story wipes-out, scrap it and start another
3. "If anyone feels that a story has screwed-up, kill it and start another!"
4. Optionally follow with Word-at-a-Time to practise abandoning ideas
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** "Never persist if there's no joy!" Compare to Theatresports players dragging scenes hunting an ending laugh. Organized boredom trains students to continue uninteresting activity. Watch self-run groups and restart joyless ones. Asked "Are you enjoying this?" players say "No we're not!" sounding astonished, as if they hadn't realized

### 9. USING A COMMITTEE

**Players:** Committee of 3 or 4 volunteers controlling, performers being controlled, remaining class as audience
**Setup:** Committee seated in front of the class, facing the acting area
**Goal:** Dramatize the opposition between audience and performer
**Rules:**
1. Committee controls the players
2. Rest of the class applauds suggestions they like, says "Mmmmmm" non-committally to adequate ones, goes "Ugh" at suggestions that thwart us
3. Sample run: "There's a knock at the door" (Mmmmmm) / "There's no one there" (boo loudly) / "There's a parcel on the welcome mat" (Applause!) / "It's another welcome mat" (Booooooooo!)
4. Committees turn devious, reducing interaction to hazards: woman at crocodile cage, "You notice a coin on the end of its snout!", keeper enters (Booooooo!)
5. Contrast target, an interactive girl-and-crocodile chain: crocodile watches you, reminds you of your old dad, you throw it a sandwich, it thanks you, you look for a loud-speaker or ventriloquist, "Yes, it's me, Jennifer!", "You can talk?", "Yes, but only you can hear me!", be fascinated by its eyes, climb the railing, it asks you to lie down beside it, feel trusting, "What do you want, Dad?", "It's about the will, Jennifer."
**Variations:** See Keep Your Promises. Skilled committees dared to be committees of one
**Coaching:** Keep changing committees. Students are astonished at the mental reversal between audience seat and committee seat. Prompt committees when stuck, repeat that storytelling is difficult, build an atmosphere where failure isn't punishing

### 10. KEEP YOUR PROMISES

**Players:** Committee plus spectators, as above
**Setup:** Interrupt a committee-controlled run after a few suggestions
**Goal:** Focus the committee on promises so it stays inside the circle of probability
**Rules:**
1. Committee feeds suggestions, e.g.: "You wake up in a tent" / "You hear drums" / "You're on safari"
2. Break in and ask spectators their expectations: "An attack by lions", "nature-photography", "encounters with ivory-poachers", "a lost tribe ruled by She-Who-Must-Be-Obeyed"
3. Feed this back so the committee meets the audience's needs rather than flailing outside the circle of probability
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Speeds the process of committee and audience becoming one

### 11. THE PERFORMER BECOMES THE COMMITTEE

**Players:** 1, or 2 for the two-character variant
**Setup:** Put someone centre-stage and ask, "Why don't you make the suggestions yourself?"
**Goal:** Diminish self-censorship by outsourcing responsibility to an internalized voice
**Rules:**
1. "Invent a bossy, parental, contemptuous voice. It has to be very officious and objectionable."
2. Ask yourself "What Comes Next?" and let the voice abuse and order you about
3. Obey it. Sample: "Go to the fridge." / "Open the door, stupid!" / "Take out the sliced bread." / "Make some toast, of course!"
4. The player may struggle against the voice, but the voice always wins: "Kiss him!" / "Do I have to?" / "Stop arguing and kiss him."
**Variations:** Sweet voices, loving voices, coaxing voices, uncertain voices, frightened voices. Scenes where two characters each give themselves instructions
**Coaching:** Seems psychotic, but wholehearted players report reduced self-censorship, the voice taking responsibility for what they do

### 12. PARADOXICAL WHAT COMES NEXT?

**Players:** 1 hero plus suggesters
**Setup:** Play What Comes Next deliberately as badly as possible
**Goal:** Insight into how stories are destroyed
**Rules:**
1. Feed the hero suggestions, each demonstrating one failure mode, labelled aloud:
[INSTANT TROUBLE] "The house explodes."
[CANCELLING] "The house assembles itself again."
[CANCELLING] "Wake up - it was all a dream!"
[BEING ORIGINAL] "Your head disappears."
[CANCELLING] "You grow a new one!"
[SIDETRACKING] "A woman rushes in the door!"
[CANCELLING] "She falls out of the window."
[BEING NEGATIVE] "She breaks her leg."
[GAGGING] "She makes no bones about it!"
[JOINING] "You break your leg."
**Variations:** Same three as What Comes Next variants: heroes adding own ideas, extra hero questions, presenter questioning the audience
**Coaching:** Use as diagnostic anatomy of cancellation, sidetrack, gag, and joining

### 13. NON-SEQUENTIAL LISTS

**Players:** Solo drill, or group
**Setup:** None, just list nouns fast
**Goal:** Break automatic chaining and threaten automatic self-censorship, warming up for Link the Items
**Rules:**
1. List nouns as quickly as possible
2. Sequential chains feel natural: "Cat, Dog, Kennel, House, Table, Chair, Sofa..."
3. Disconnected objects feel unnatural: "House, Waterfall, Mandrake-root, Grinling Gibbons, Wellington Boot, Forest Fire, Grandfather Clock, One Parsec, Margarine, Shirley Temple"
4. Expect sweating and mental skid: "what has Margarine got to do with Shirley Temple?"
**Variations:** See performance version
**Coaching:** The inability to censor such lists makes the games feel enticingly risky

### 14. PERFORMANCE NON-SEQUENTIAL LISTS

**Players:** 1 performer, a timekeeper (usually the Commentator), spectators counting
**Setup:** Challenge format between teams
**Goal:** Name maximum disconnected objects in 20 seconds
**Rules:**
1. Team A challenges: "We challenge you to see how many disconnected objects a player can name in twenty seconds." Team B: "We accept!"
2. Spectators count each noun in unison: "Cabbage!" / "ONE!" / "The North Wall of the Eiger!" / "TWO!" / "A twenty-dollar bill!" / "THREE!" / "A sleeping princess!" / "FOUR!" / "A... a... a... a castle!" everyone boos the perceived princess-castle link
3. Timekeeper reports elapsed time, next player comes centre-stage
4. After six or seven items most players contort as if wrestling invisible opponents and give up
**Variations:** Improvisers from both teams play together, shouting words alternately, trying to avoid connections
**Coaching:** Ensure enough laughter to dispel anxiety. Really a warm-up for Link the Items

### 15. LINK THE ITEMS

**Players:** 2 (or demonstrated solo in writing)
**Setup:** Play Non-sequential Lists first
**Goal:** Connect disconnected events into coherent narrative
**Rules:**
1. One player states disconnected events, the second connects them
2. Example pair 1: "John woke up one morning to find that the sky was filled with black clouds. He looked at the bookshelf and saw that his Bible was missing." becomes "Remembering the prophecy that a black cloud would annihilate wrong-doers, John ran to get his New Testament, but every Bible in the city had turned to ash and the smoke was blotting out the sun."
3. Example pair 2: "Father Christmas was angry because the elves were on strike. A whale was seen with two heads." becomes "'No more toys for earth-children, until they do something about the pollution!' said the Chief Elf."
4. Three-item version: "Susan was having trouble finishing her homework. Charley was battering a slot machine with his fist. A band of bagpipers began marching along the avenue." Linked: Charley forgot his ear-plugs, battered the ear-plug machine that swallowed his money, and the bagpipers' savage sound triggers his werewolf transformation, snarling in a Scottish accent, wolf-hair pushing out
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Model the connection aloud yourself before expecting it of students

### 16. VERBAL CHASE

**Players:** 2
**Setup:** One asks non-sequential questions, the other answers and links them
**Goal:** Justify arbitrary leaps in dialogue. Leads naturally to the Expert Game (p. 123) and the Boris Game
**Rules:**
1. Questioner fires unrelated questions, answerer must connect them:
"Why did you open the umbrella in the living room?" / "To bring bad luck!" / "And the egg whisk?" / "Suicide weapon!"
2. Longer run: "Where were you at ten minutes after midnight?" / "In bed." / "With what fruit?" / "A ugli-fruit." / "And the bananas?" / "They were for the chimp?" / "What's its T-shirt say?" / "Africa for the Africans." / "And the space-ship?" / "He left it double-parked."
3. Common fault, questioner doing all the work: "Double-parked in front of the Mayor's office?" / "Yes!" / "And the pig was in the back seat?" / "That's right!" / "Eating the new tax regulations?" / "So it was!"
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Watch for the questioner carrying everything. Gateway game to Expert and Boris

### 17. THE BORIS GAME

**Players:** 3 roles: interrogator, victim, Boris (an invisible eight-foot thug, sometimes another player voicing screams)
**Setup:** Introduce as "advanced", putting students on their mettle and absolving screw-ups. Training wheels phase: interrogator strikes failures with an airship balloon, 2 to 2.5 feet long, several inches in diameter, not floppy animal balloons
**Goal:** Packaging for Verbal Chase. Arbitrary leaps must be justified instantly, training free association and self-incrimination
**Rules:**
1. Interrogator asks disconnected questions, prisoner must make sense of them: "Ms Ferguson, is it?" / "Look, I just came in here to use the toilet facilities." / "May I ask why you were washing that money?" / "I was counting the notes and some fell in the washbasin."
2. Answers designed to dodge get punished: balloon strike, both players yelling loudly: "Auuughh! All right! I'll tell! I'll tell! I was trying to wash the blue dye off!" / "And the scorpions?" / "Er... they... er... Auuughh!" / "I threw them into the ticket-seller's booth so that I could snatch the money!"
3. Yelling and writhing in pain makes it easier to spew ideas from the unconscious. Forcing incriminating answers produces bizarre material
4. Remove the balloon, add invisible Boris. Interrogator: "Bring him in, Boris!" Howls of terror, eight-foot Boris forces Gunnar onstage, thumps him into a chair
5. Interrogator begins courteously: "Perhaps you can tell us where you were on Thursday the 16th, sir?" Unsatisfactory answer triggers: "Help him, Boris!" Boris twists nose or arm or hurls victim at ceiling
6. Acceptable answers restrain Boris: "Well?" / "I was - I was at home!" / "With a dead horse?" / "It was a surprise for the wife!" Katrina restrains Boris, continues
7. Both players track exactly where imaginary Boris stands at all times. If they stare differently, interrogator introduces Igor, Boris's ill-natured twin
8. Interrogator rejects answers that sidetrack, cancel, or seem too gaggy, shaping a narrative
**Variations:** The Classic Boris formal rules below. Settings once mastered: bowling alleys, royal bedrooms, dungeons below Swiss finishing schools
**Coaching:** Victims incriminate themselves so Boris won't abuse them, which is why beginners volunteer as Heroes resisting torture, nothing happens till they confess. Boris must terrify enough that victims admit absolutely anything. Weak interrogators micro-manage: "Pull his nose, Boris!", "Rip off his leg!", better to blind-offer "Help him, Boris!" and let victims choose torments. A University of Waterloo Boris broke a collarbone, ambulance called, generally screams beat gymnastics. Boris is invisible so cruelty is self-inflicted, letting audiences laugh unreservedly

### 18. THE CLASSIC BORIS

**Players:** Interrogator, victim, invisible Boris
**Setup:** As Boris Game, formal rules as first devised
**Goal:** Full-length interrogation narrative, story over acrobatics
**Rules:**
1. Ask Boris to bring in the victim, or just shout "Next!" Victim hurls herself onstage or pretends to be frog-marched, as if propelled by the eight-foot Boris
2. Start politely. Unsatisfactory answers earn "Help him, Boris!" or "Jog her memory, Boris!" Victim howls in fake agony, reserving gymnastics until later in the scene
3. At unpredictable intervals, perhaps just once, interrogator says "Let's recapitulate," reviews the material, adds information, tries to make sense of the rigmarole
4. Present the victim with an object, a handkerchief, a shoe, even an audience member: "Perhaps this will jog your memory!" Victim expresses appalled and guilty recognition, weaves the object into the story
5. Force some resolution and/or call in the next victim
6. Sample flow: club scene, punishment-collar batteries dead, "Club? I came in here to use the telephone!" / "Help him, Boris!" / "All right! I'm lying! I'm... I'm a new member!" / "Weren't you black-balled?" / "No! Yes, I was! I was!" ... "So how did you get a letter of invitation?" / "I forged it!" (good answer, incriminating) ... Flexie-Boy Printing Set childhood, Sammy the Goldfish educated with books against the bowl, migraine, vet flushed him, substitute fish claimed miracle cure, taught to yodel, honour place in the billiard-hall tank, then the handbill: "Marvo the Magician... And his yodelling goldfish!" ... "just who is this Marvo?" / "Me! It's me! What use denying it?" ... launderette underwear kept in chest of drawers, worn as part of the act
**Variations:** None beyond setting changes
**Coaching:** If the interrogator enjoys the victim's flights of fancy they are allowed to continue. Emphasis must be on story or the game has little sustaining power. Very exhilarating, like skiing in total darkness

**Escape Hatches (for Boris):** 1. Implicate the interrogator: "I gave the money to you!" / "Me?" / "You think I didn't recognize you behind that moustache?" 2. Implicate Boris: "Boris tossed me up!" / "I'm sorry Boris, I had to tell him." 3. Attack Boris and hurl him about, another player grabbing the mike to supply screams. 4. The Androcles hatch: realize you once removed a thorn from Boris's foot

### 19. KEYBOARD GAME

**Players:** 1 typist plus actors who enact the story (they may add ideas)
**Setup:** Typist sits upstage and to the side, angled toward the audience but with a clear view of the action, typing at an imaginary keyboard and speaking the story aloud. Split-attention game, like the Hat-Game (p. 156)
**Goal:** Generate a fully acted story through spoken narration, sharing attention between typing and staging
**Rules:**
1. Speak the story as you type. At first the keyboard takes most of your attention, soon you only indicate it occasionally
2. Title: usual to ask the audience, but you may just type whatever occurs, deleting and retyping if disliked. Reject sabotage "originalities" like The Hind-leg of the Drunk Porcupine. Never accept a genre or title that fails to inspire
3. Actors first repeat or mouth the typist's dialogue, later add their own ideas, but never take over entirely
4. Structural rules: place the hero in a stable environment; establish a purpose and/or tragic flaw; optional scene change (home, work, movie); hero interacts involving suffering and chaos; achieve an ending by reincorporating shelved material, trashing the hero or creating new stability
5. Worked example, Harold's Adventure: type "Harold woke up one morning feeling particularly well. The sunlight was streaming into his room..." A player becomes Harold, hears birdsong, says "What a great day! I think I'll phone in sick!" Implicit promises: day off plus adventure, so he phones the boss feigning illness
6. Type a tragic flaw: "Harold's overriding fear was his dread of losing control." Mirror check, neat clothes, buttons sewn tightly, correct hair, sighs happily. Suspense planted
7. Weather was stressed, so outdoor promise: "Harold decided to visit the zoo." A frightened typist cancels (stove left on), bridges (misses the bus), or sidetracks (remembered appointment)
8. At the zoo: "Harold found himself all alone in..." pick any area, "...in the Ape House." Interaction: "Harold stared at Guy, and Guy stared back." An improviser leaps on as the gorilla. Moving to another cage would deny the promised interaction, so break the routine: "Harold noticed a gleam of metal in the gorilla's fur."
9. Actors may take over: zip-fastener realized, acrobat unzips from the suit, love, jobs as gorilla impersonators. Spectators want more chaos given Harold's flaw, so "delete" the zip-fastener: gorilla extends a finger, Harold touches it, finds himself inside the cage, covered in fur, watching his own body beat its chest outside the bars, inhabited by the gorilla's mind. Orderly world becomes disorderly, promise kept
10. Endings via reincorporation: the boss arrives at the zoo (Harold lied about illness), abuses the gorilla/Harold, gets thrown about. Or gorilla becomes lumberjack, Harold famous as the gorilla making paper aeroplanes. Or Harold wakes, all a dream, gorilla enters with breakfast tray. Or supply the missing purpose: Harold decides to rescue Guy and return him to Africa, stolen zoo-keeper uniform, tailored parcel, "Today was the day when he would return Guy the Gorilla to the wild!"

**Escape Hatches (when inspiration fails):**
1. Bring the character to you: "Suddenly Harold heard typing. There was a small door low in the wall. He opened it and saw another gorilla typing away..." Then type "Harold stepped through the door and..." Harold can complain, recognize you as an old friend, criticize the story, or read it aloud from the screen. Another player wakes up in the morning, lights fade, ersatz circularity achieved
2. Forest hero finds you typing behind a rock, in a hut, inside a hollow tree, or tiny under a leaf
3. Characterize yourself: "A drunken degenerate was drooling over a keyboard...", "She peered into the kennel and saw Fido typing away...", a bee in his shirt pocket typing on a miniature computer
4. Type "Something unexpected happened!" and let Harold solve it
5. Ask the audience "What comes next?"
6. Substitution sign: horizontal flat hand on vertical hand making a T shape, someone replaces you
7. Pretend to delete the last few sentences, audiences love characters deleting themselves
8. Hero reads a letter, diary, book, or newspaper aloud, creep silently away, hero becomes storyteller, or fade lights
9. Enter the scene as a character, Harold's boss perhaps, Harold rushes to the keyboard, keep alternating
10. Remember something shelved earlier and feed it back in

**Variations:** Typed by Captain Ahab, by the wolf that ate Red Riding Hood, by a vengeful God. Genre request: film noir, science fiction, Western, genres don't wreck keyboard stories but never accept one that doesn't inspire. "Dear Diary": diary writing, suicide-note composition, Captain Kirk updating the Enterprise log
**Coaching:** Fear of blanking turns the game into an ordeal, one improviser needed "about a year" before daring it, having seen it performed without learning its escape hatches
