## 1. The Die Game
**Players:** Four, five or six improvisers facing the audience, plus a conductor; the audience plays too.
**Setup:** A stage-facing-the-audience arrangement and an agreed story title.
**Goal:** Warming up the spectators.
**Rules:** 1. The conductor explains that any player pointed at will begin or continue a story, and that if he/she hesitates, is ungrammatical, or makes some other error, the audience should shout 'Die', forcing the miscreant to 'commit suicide'; the last one left 'alive' wins. 2. Rehearse the audience in shouting 'Die' until they agree to be really loud (shouting makes them more responsive). 3. Begin the story, with the conductor trying to throw the players by pointing at them unexpectedly and lingering on any individual who seems about to crack up. 4. Players who are to 'die' will often ask for an object to die with, e.g. 'Can you name an implement you might find in a kitchen?', someone shouts 'Cheese grater!', and the player cheese-grates himself or herself to death (the audience laughs at the stupidity); it's best to find your own way of dying, and if you're stuck perhaps the sound imp will provide a rapidly approaching train or whatever.
**Variations:** Genre variant: each player is given a genre such as sci-fi, religion, romance, Western, and the story passes between genres; Johnstone's sample runs sci-fi ('As the spaceship approached Beta Four there was no sign of life from the colonists. Captain Skoog would be glad to be rid of the cargo of...'), religious ('...monks who had come to set up a branch of their order. His...'), romance ('...handsome profile assumed a masterful severity, as Communications Officer Ulla-Karin failed to detect any answering transmission...'), Western ('...from the Crossed Tentacle ranch far below where Tex had just wrestled one of the local Xangs into submission. The branding-iron was hissing into its body armour as it stared skywards. Following its gaze, Tex saw the bright star hovering over the foothills and...'), religious again ('...all over the prairie the rough soma-chewing Xang-boys knelt in awe, believing the atomic fire in the sky to be the sign they had been...'), then a romance player dithers ('Er... er...') and the audience yells 'DIE!' with relish. Second variant: no genres are agreed on beforehand and the conductor shouts out a genre as he points; Johnstone wouldn't recommend this.
**Coaching:** Any game that gets the audience to yell loudly is useful, but Johnstone would hate to see the Die Game in show after show; use it as a warm-up game, not as a 'closer'.

## 2. Emotional Goals
**Players:** Start with a whole group, then pairs; works best with two players, or two players and a 'passenger' (add more and it's difficult to follow).
**Setup:** No props needed.
**Goal:** Training gradual emotional transitions ('gradualism'), modulating from one state to another in tiny steps.
**Rules:** 1. Players start with one emotion (or 'condition') and modulate to another, for example sad to happy, proud to ashamed, silly to intelligent. 2. Begin by asking a group to move from, say, 'interest' to 'panic'; perhaps they're so engrossed watching creatures trapped in a rock pool that they become marooned by the incoming tide. 3. Try various emotions, stressing the need to make gradual transitions. 4. Then have pairs play the game. 5. Then have each player choose his/her own emotions: one may be heading from terror to lust, while another goes from panic to calm.
**Variations:** None given.
**Coaching:** Asked to change from 'anger' to 'affection', most students start mildly peeved and take fifteen seconds to get totally enraged, yet they're supposed to be heading towards affection, not away from it; advise them to begin with whatever intensity they can muster and start diminishing it immediately. Encourage transitions by tiny steps: if you photographed them at fifteen-second intervals you'd want to be able to shuffle the prints and then lay them out in the order in which they were taken. This concept of 'gradualism' is easier to grasp if they've already played the Tempo Game (p. 207). Changing violently is dramatic, but changing slowly and continuously can also grip the attention (study Garbo, Vanessa Redgrave, Brando, and similar great actors). Footnote: Emotional Goals are described by Viola Spolin in Improvisation for the Theatre.

## 3. Endowments
**Players:** One improviser sent out of earshot, plus scene partners who endow him/her, with suggestions from the audience.
**Setup:** Nothing physical; get a 'profession and two physical qualities' from the audience.
**Goal:** Communication through indirect endowment; useful in the early days 'while we were still flailing about'.
**Rules:** 1. Send an improviser out of earshot and get a 'profession and two physical qualities' from the audience, perhaps a 'teacher' who is 'blind' and has 'a huge nose'. 2. Fetch the improviser back and play a scene in which his/her partners endow him with these items, using gibberish; alternatively play the scene in English while giving the information indirectly. 3. Sample English dialogue: 'Come in. Careful! Keep your head straight! I suppose you find the door a bit narrow!' / 'It's always nice to visit you little people!' (instead of 'a huge nose' they've conveyed 'gigantism', and the spectators laugh at the misunderstanding); 'I'll drop a peanut. Perhaps you'd like to pick it up? No need to bend down!' / 'I'm an elephant!' (boos and laughter); 'I've got a big nose!' (applause); 'I'm afraid I forgot my homework.' / 'Do you want to copy mine?' (laughter at the error); 'I'm a teacher with big nose!' (applause); 'Careful! There's a desk to your right.' / 'I'm blind!'
**Variations:** Variant to avoid: some players become 'crippled' to communicate a limp, or get hiccups to convey that their partner has indigestion; do this and endowments aren't worth playing. It's reasonable to reach for something on the ground and say, 'You dropped your white stick,' but it's moronic to signal the need for your partner to become 'blind' by becoming 'blind' yourself. Chain Endowments (not a game to see more than once): several players are sent out of earshot while the endowments are being decided, then 'A' endows 'qualities' on to 'B', who conveys his understanding (misunderstanding) to 'C', and so on; the information becomes increasingly garbled and the audience laugh.
**Coaching:** Johnstone prefers the gibberish version. Endowments guarantee a laugh, but it's like shooting fish in a barrel.

## 4. Freeze Games
**Players:** Two or more players start an activity; others rush in on the freeze.
**Setup:** No props needed.
**Goal:** Claimed to develop the players' imaginations; in practice it teaches players to be original, and to sidetrack and cancel.
**Rules:** 1. The players start an activity, such as directing traffic. 2. Someone shouts 'Freeze!' and runs into the scene to change the activity into some unrelated but visually similar one. 3. Example sequence: two people are pushing a boulder; a freeze changes them into three sailors tossing about in a storm; the next freeze changes them into four downhill skiers. People laugh because they like transformations.
**Variations:** None given.
**Coaching:** These visual puns are among the most popular of all improvisation games because they almost always get a laugh and there's no risk that anything might happen; Johnstone saw excellent examples in workshops at Chicago's Second City, but many less destructive games develop imagination too. They were never played in his classes at the Studio, and never by the Theatre Machine, but you might get challenged to one, and any game can be a straw to grab at when you're desperate. Why train students to kill stories? Morphing from 'playing the accordion' to 'stretching chest-expanders' to 'firing an arrow' to being 'nailed on a cross' gets admiration, but it's like warming yourself by burning the floorboards. We need coherent sequences like 'firing an arrow', and 'killing an animal', and 'discovering a collar around its neck that says "Property of the Emperor"'.

## 5. Guess the Phrase
**Players:** One guesser (Martin in the example), scene partners, and the audience supplying the phrase.
**Setup:** Stage space for a run of short scenes; nothing else needed.
**Goal:** It improves communication skills.
**Rules:** 1. Send Martin out of earshot and get the audience to agree on a well-known phrase, song title, or whatever. 2. He returns and takes part in scenes that attempt to convey the phrase to him; his partners are not allowed to mention any of the keywords in the title. 3. For 'birds of a feather, flock together': a few players flap imaginary wings and start discussing migration; Martin becomes a bird and they start billing and cooing together; he guesses 'The early bird gets the worm?' and everyone laughs or groans. 4. A new scene begins instantly, Martin shown around a baronial hall by the owner: 'Shot and mounted most of these birds myself. I've got a complete set of Darwin's finches in the conservatory...'; Martin guesses 'You've feathered your own nest?'. 5. A duck-hunting scene begins: 'Look at that! They're packed so close that I can shoot them all in one burst!'; Martin says hopefully, 'The early worm gets the bird?'. 6. A new scene establishes him as the newest member of an ornithology club; the players hand him binoculars: 'Amazing! Look how the grebes are avoiding the loons!'; Martin says, 'Birds of a feather flock together,' and everyone cheers. 7. Typical phrases: Let sleeping dogs lie; Love thy neighbour; Don't bite off more than you can chew; Don't judge a book by its cover; The gods help those who help themselves. Typical song titles: 'Like a Virgin'; 'Que sera sera'.
**Variations:** None given.
**Coaching:** Obscure proverbs or song titles are useless, but a well-known title like 'Like a Virgin' can suggest scenes in the back seat of a car, or human sacrifices. Each wrong guess is followed instantly by a fresh scene attacking the phrase from another angle.

## 6. The No 'S' Game
**Players:** Scene players; the audience listens for violations.
**Setup:** Agree on a forbidden letter before the scene.
**Goal:** Forces the players to attend to what they're saying; can also convince them of the value of failure.
**Rules:** 1. The players must avoid words that use an agreed letter. 2. Don't select letters that are rarely used, and don't let beginners select vowels. 3. Play the scene; some players are so used to leaving their mouths on automatic that they'll begin by saying 'Please, sir...' or 'Sit down, Susan...' and lose instantly, to the delight of the spectators. 4. If the players are making no errors, tell them to speed up, because there's no point in playing this game unless someone loses.
**Variations:** Variant: replace anyone who uses a word with 'S' in it and let the scene continue. Destructive variant: each player is asked to avoid a different letter, even though the spectators have enough trouble listening for just one forbidden letter; an excellent example of the way simple games can be ruined by adding complexity.
**Coaching:** Johnstone invented the game mid-lecture when extemporizing ways to interfere with verbalization. The spectators are longing for a player to screw up so that they can laugh and feel superior, hence the lesson in the value of failure.

## 7. A Scene without...
**Players:** Scene players, with the audience supplying the condition.
**Setup:** No props needed.
**Goal:** A filler scene game driven by audience suggestion.
**Rules:** 1. Ask the audience to complete the phrase 'a scene without...'; for example: 'a scene without scruples', or 'without air', or 'without guilt', or 'without a friend', or 'without a brain', or 'without hope', or 'without talent'. 2. Play the scene under the suggested deprivation.
**Variations:** None given.
**Coaching:** It's okay to ask for suggestions here, because even the most malevolent audience member finds it difficult to be destructive in this context.

## 8. Sideways Scenes
**Players:** Two or more performers; optional 'handlers' recruited from the audience for held positions.
**Setup:** The rear of the stage treated as the floor and the 'fourth wall' between actors and audience treated as the ceiling; a desk and two chairs set on their sides with their 'feet' against the back wall (or rear curtain); optionally a Velcro-rigged mug, a bed or sofa stood upright against the rear wall, a fly costume.
**Goal:** Illusion play with redirected gravity; it dates from the class in which Johnstone set out to find every possible way a scene could be played (backward scenes were invented at the same time).
**Rules:** 1. Pretend that gravity is pulling the players away from the audience instead of towards the centre of the earth, as though the audience was looking down through the ceiling of an office. 2. The boss lies on the floor with the top of his head towards the audience and his feet against the rear wall; he struggles out of his coat and 'hangs' it on an imaginary hook on the floor some five feet away from the rear wall. 3. He levers himself towards the desk, 'rowing' himself along with his lower arm while moving his feet backward and forward along the wall, which gives the illusion that he's walking; he 'sits' at the desk pressing his bottom against its sideways seat, takes papers out of the sideways drawer, and 'rests' his feet on the sideways desk. 4. Any doors in the back wall are treated as trap-doors: Perkins emerges wriggling on to the stage as if climbing up a ladder, holds the boss's coffee mug sideways, and 'walks' along the back wall to place the mug on the vertical surface of the sideways desk; either it stays (rigged with Velcro) or the boss berates Perkins when it crashes to the 'wall' (i.e. on to the stage floor): 'How dare you throw my favourite mug at the wall just because I halved your salary!' 5. If the boss is shot, he becomes dead by standing in some crooked position against the rear wall (perhaps the police will tape around his outline). 6. Fly scene example: Dennis Cahill 'buzzed about' wearing a fly costume and 'landed' on the 'ceiling' (the fourth wall): 'It's that confounded fly again.' / 'I'll get the spray from the cupboard, sir.' They sprayed him and he 'fell' away from the audience and crash-landed on to his attacker in terminal agony.
**Variations:** Try a levitation scene in which a guru edges towards the audience as if floating to the ceiling; or a spiderman scene; or present an interview with an acrobat; or stand a bed upright against the rear wall and have the players stand against it and hold the bedding up in front of them; or a love scene on a sofa stood on its end with the lovers held in position, which took about ten 'handlers' who rushed out from the audience but was worth the effort.
**Coaching:** Excellent for very occasional use. Sideways scenes can be hysterically funny, but Johnstone has never seen other groups use them; dirty floors may be one reason. His company washes the stage before each performance, but he remembers a space in New York's Bowery district where anything that touched the floor was soiled by an oily black paste.

## 9. Yes-But
**Players:** Two players: Kurt asks questions, Gloria answers.
**Setup:** No props needed.
**Goal:** Demonstrates the difference between intellectual and intuitive thinking; trains unchosen answers.
**Rules:** 1. Kurt asks questions that can receive a 'yes' answer, and Gloria responds with sentences that begin with 'Yes, but...': 'Are you closed?' / 'Yes, but do come in!'; 'Are you the animal doctor?' / 'Yes, but I don't do snakes!'; 'I'm afraid this is an emergency - that lump is the professor.' / 'Yes, but...'. And so on. 2. One common way to end the game is by having both players use 'Yes, but...' sentences: 'You'll have to go in after him!' / 'Yes, but then I might be digested!'; 'Yes, but I'll get a rope to tie around you.'; 'Yes, but I'm no good at knots.'; 'Yes, but I was a boy scout!' You can usually fade the lights somewhere about here; keep such scenes short. 3. As a teaching demonstration, Johnstone first answers students' 'yes questions' while 'staying safe', thinking up answers before opening his mouth: 'How old are you?' / 'I'm sorry, I have to be able to give a "yes" answer.'; 'Are you a hundred years old?' / 'Yes, but I'm quite fit for my age.'; 'Do you read much?' / 'Yes, but mostly about current events.'; 'Are you interested in politics?' / 'Yes, but I'm not sure who to vote for.' 4. He then admits he's been playing safe, offers to be braver, and this time shouts 'Yes! But!' vigorously and loudly with no idea what will follow: 'Are you a hundred years old?' / 'Yes! But I feel like ninety-eight!'; 'Did you vote in the election.' / 'Yes! But if it made any difference they wouldn't let me!'; 'Have you ever run for parliament?' / 'Yes! But I kept running.'
**Variations:** After Yes-But try the more positive Yes-And.
**Coaching:** Deciding what to say before you speak leads to planned answers, but shouting 'Yes! But...' with a blank mind drags out 'unchosen' answers which are almost always more entertaining. If you use the game in performance, to add variety (why else?), keep it short. Source: learned from a spectator at a Theatre Machine performance who had studied Viola Spolin's Improvisation for the Theatre, a book describing work in the Compass Players and Second City tradition.
