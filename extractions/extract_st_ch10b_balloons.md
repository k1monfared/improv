# Keith Johnstone, Impro for Storytellers, chapter 10 Procedures part 2, balloon and audience games (source: /tmp/opencode/johnstone_storytellers.txt, lines 11071-11444)

## 1. Hitting with Balloons
**Players:** Pairs (soon framed as master and servant), or a trainer working with a student
**Setup:** Long 'airship' balloons that inflate to a couple of feet long and inches in diameter, ideally about a yard long and three or four inches across as the Theatre Machine's were, not the long thin balloons used for making balloon animals. Keep a large stock at the sides of the stage and backstage, the Theatre Machine had a hundred or more, sometimes stuck to the walls by static electricity, and sometimes destroyed most of them in a single performance.
**Goal:** Invigorating, safe slapstick. Balloons are brightly coloured, move strangely, and are 'safe' and 'dangerous' at the same time, exploding loudly but harmlessly, and the long ones are subliminally erotic. They are an excellent replacement for slap-sticks (two thin strips of slightly separated board that made a loud slap), which required padded victims and still risked hitting with the edge. Hitting with balloons gave Master-Servant scenes a new vitality.
**Rules:** 1. You strike someone with a long balloon and he/she reacts as if struck by an iron bar, but recovers a lot sooner. 2. No padding is needed, and if the balloon bursts you can simply be handed another one. 3. Keep playing, treating every blow as an iron-bar blow with quick recovery, and restock balloons as they burst.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** A Need for Caution: some balloons contain a pinch of talcum powder to stop the rubber sticking to itself. Deposits of talc are usually contaminated with asbestos (one commercial sample of baby powder was found to be 38 per cent asbestos), so if you find talc in your mouth or on your lips after inflating a balloon, wash your mouth out. Consider buying a balloon pump. If a student has a fear of balloons, take the class outside where there are no walls to reflect the sound, and note that so much laughter is released by balloon-hitting that nervous students should be easy to desensitize.

## 2. Hitting with Entry
**Players:** Pairs, first striking their own forearms, then each other
**Setup:** Airship balloons
**Goal:** Teaches hitting through the object rather than at the surface. Human beings instinctively strike at the surface of other people as a way to assert status without doing serious damage, so entry has to be taught or the balloons will just be tapped against the partner.
**Rules:** 1. 'Hit your forearms with the balloon. Try to burst it with the force of the blow!' 2. Notice that the intention to burst the balloon forces a firm grip (instead of just letting it waggle), and that it becomes obvious the balloon cannot hurt anyone however hard you strike. 3. 'Now strike your partner with the intention of smashing the balloon right through them.' 4. Carry this into Master-Servant scenes, where masters who seem 'mediocre' become demonic, and inspired, if they hit with entry.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Entry means hitting through the object, as in those martial arts demonstrations where the head is accelerated to a point beyond a concrete block, trusting that it's the concrete that will shatter.

## 3. Restraint
**Players:** Master-servant pairs, with a trainer intervening physically and verbally
**Setup:** One balloon per master
**Goal:** Converts beginner hitting (beating carpets, suitable only for ending a Benny Hill sketch) into dramatically valuable blows, each with a separate build. If masters struggle not to hit their servants, the balloon becomes a gauge that exquisitely records the master's feelings.
**Rules:** 1. 'Give each hit a separate build-up!' 2. When masters try to deliver a shower of follow-on blows, grab their arms if necessary: 'That's enough! One hit only. Now beg your servant's forgiveness!' 3. Play it so the balloon seems to be getting enraged while the master tries to calm it down: hold it behind the back as if to keep it out of the argument, let it vibrate and take little swipes at the air as it works its way round to the front again, raise it to strike and then force it down with both hands as if trying to press it under water. 4. Some masters beat themselves in their rage, or destroy their balloon, only to be instantly handed another. 5. The moment to hit is when the balloon can no longer contain its violence: a good hit is like a sneeze, something that comes after a 'build'. 6. If the hit is delayed too long and 'misses the wave', let the tension subside so you can start building to a fresh climax. 7. Once you've hit your servant (against your better judgment), apologize, make amends, and start building the balloon's rage again until it is impelled to lash out once more.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Masters can easily make themselves enraged (they love negative emotions) but then their balloons seem quite 'dead'.

## 4. The Wheel of Tension
**Players:** Anyone for the clapping demonstration, then a master with a servant while the trainer controls the release
**Setup:** Balloons for the master-servant phase
**Goal:** Awareness of the inner rhythm we are normally unaware of, and training the moment of hitting to be unpredictable rather than metronomic.
**Rules:** 1. Clap your hands, then wait (without counting) perhaps twenty or thirty seconds until there comes a moment when you know it's time to clap again, as if a huge wheel were revolving slowly at the edge of your consciousness and prompting one clap at each rotation. 2. Trainers interfere with this regularity, insisting that the moment of hitting should be unpredictable, and teach the skill by having the moment of release decided by the trainer. 3. Drive a master through a coached build such as: 'Not yet. Get angrier. Don't hit him. HIT NOW! Once! ONLY ONCE! Back, back. Help her up, dust her down, tidy her clothing. Now remember why you hit her and vibrate the balloon a bit. Calm the balloon down. Pour her a brandy, notice that half the brandy is missing! Get enraged but try not to show it. Ask where the brandy has gone. Realize that your servant has been stealing your brandy. Let the balloon go berserk. DON'T HIT HER! IT'S TOO LATE. Let the emotion subside a little. HIT HER AGAIN! NOW HIT HER AGAIN... Ask her to get you your tranquillizers. Make her swallow a handful...' and so on (see Spasms, p. 314).
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** None given

## 5. Balloon Fights
**Players:** Whole group for the elimination fights, then master-servant pairs
**Setup:** Airship balloons, open space, and mats or carpet once real falling begins
**Goal:** Desensitizes balloon-shy performers, and trains balance, physical control, breath control, and the receiving of blows.
**Rules:** 1. Set up 'sword-fights' with the balloons, en masse, until only one person is left standing, everyone stabbed by a balloon counting as 'dead'. 2. Try sword-fights in slow-motion, and listen for the 'tap' of a balloon hitting someone, which means it was speeding up to tap the surface. Side-coach: 'Slow-motion! Think of pressing the balloon into the body! Keep the centre of the body in motion, not just the arm that you intend to strike with, or you'll look like a railway signal.' 3. If you're slain, be smashed to the floor in slow-motion and bounce a few times, but don't speed up, there shouldn't be a sound as you hit the floor. 4. Place blows so they can be 'received': don't strike the knees from in front, strike from the rear so the legs can buckle, keep moving the balloon after contact with increasing pressure, thrust in slow-motion at the stomach so the servant grasps the balloon and bends over and can grip it until it bursts or makes a horrid wrenching noise as it's pulled away, strike at one shoulder so the servant can spin around, or hit straight down so the servant staggers about clutching her head or falls straight to the floor, but avoid the side of the head because a balloon must not explode beside someone's ear. 5. For servants who sillyly speed up to evade the slow-motion balloon, teach them to grimace with increasing horror as it moves inexorably towards their face, holding their heads still if necessary. 6. Get students to play scenes or 'fence' in slow-motion using slow dialogue: 'Taaaake thaaaaaaat yooouuuuu swiiinnnnne!' answered by 'Oooooohhh, noooooooo. Auuuuuggghhhhh!' 7. Once slow-motion hitting is mastered, ask students to speed up their interactions and have hit servants fall over, using mats or carpet, letting fit and healthy students do spectacular falls, but employing a really good tumbling instructor if you want them to stay fit and healthy, and remembering that the cries of pain and the disorientation matter more than the actual fall. 8. Most servants prefer to stand just beyond hitting range (as if the balloons really were iron bars), so tell the masters, 'Make the servants come to you! Don't go to them!' and tell the servants, 'Stand close. Try to be within three feet of the master. You're allowed to duck when you're swiped at, but you're not to avoid the blow by increasing the distance.'
**Variations:** Mass elimination sword-fights, slow-motion fights and scenes with slow dialogue, and full-speed fights with falls on mats.
**Coaching:** Moving and falling in slow-motion is excellent for balance and physical control, and the slow dialogue is excellent for breath control. There's something comical (and perhaps political) about the servant who tries to avoid being hit, and yet who always stands in the line of fire.

## 6. A Master-Servant Balloon-hitting Scene
**Players:** Two, a master (Sir) and a servant (Perkins)
**Setup:** A balloon for the master, room to strike and dive to the floor
**Goal:** Keeps the threat of violence alive so the scene stays charged, and holds both master and servant to the present moment.
**Rules:** 1. Improvise on this model: Sir calls 'Perkins!', Perkins answers 'Sir?', and Sir demands 'Didn't I forbid you ever to be taller than me?' 2. Perkins argues back (all the servants going about on their hands and knees is stupid, the maids can't make the beds unless you go upstairs with them), the balloon becoming extremely agitated, ending on 'just because you're short!' 3. 'Short!' Sir strikes Perkins to the floor, is overcome with remorse ('Good God! What a brute I am'), helps Perkins up, accepts reassurance ('No harm done, sir'), confesses 'I've such a terrible temper', and when his balloon begins to vibrate snaps 'Are you contradicting me?' 4. On the answer the balloon becomes more alive, Sir sits suddenly on the floor, Perkins dives to the carpet, Sir stands and claims 'Just testing you, Perkins!', the balloon calming, and asks for the Increase Your Height book from the top shelf. 5. As the balloon trembles again and takes little swipes in the air while Sir appears to be restraining it forcibly ('I don't keep a dog and bark myself'), Perkins offers 'Perhaps you'd like to climb up with me, sir?' then produces a book 'like a flash'. 6. Sir discovers it's Ballroom Dancing for Midgets from the bottom shelf ('Don't lie to me!'), smashes Perkins down mad with rage, is horrified by his own violence, and asks 'Are you all right, old chap?', Perkins answering 'Oh yes, sir, just startled me a bit', and so on.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** It helps to roar or shriek or scream when hitting or being hit. Even if the master always manages to restrain the balloon, the threat keeps the scene alive and holds both master and servant to the present moment.

## 7. The Spontaneous Balloon
**Players:** Any performer wielding a balloon (typically the master)
**Setup:** An airship balloon tied with a 'gimmicked' knot: loop the neck as usual but insert the mouth only about a third of the way through the loop, so the pressure of the air and the friction of the rubber make a tight seal that you can still deflate at will. Inserted less than a millimetre or so into the loop, it becomes a spontaneous balloon that might discharge at any moment.
**Goal:** Comic deflation, giving the audience great pleasure as the master is reduced to flapping a little strip of rubber impotently in the air.
**Rules:** 1. Inflate and tie the balloon with the gimmicked knot. 2. Arrange the knot so it sits at the end that hits the servant. 3. Strike: the balloon is quite likely to deflate on impact, whooshing away and leaving the master waving the limp strip of rubber.
**Variations:** Insert the mouth a third of the way into the loop for a reusable balloon you can deflate and save for another day, or less than a millimetre in for one that might discharge at any moment (in Johnstone's childhood house, balloons would suddenly whoosh about and his parents never understood why).
**Coaching:** None given

## 8. Beep-Beep
**Players:** One speaker addressing a group of from six to eight seated people
**Setup:** Seats for the group, nothing else
**Goal:** Replaces the alienating 'eye-slide' (freezing your gaze so it slides off a passing stranger, avoiding the status transaction of breaking eye contact) with 'recognition behaviour' (raising eyebrows, widening eyes, smiling, enlarged pupils), the way a rock star flashing recognition signals into a dark arena makes thousands feel 'seen'. It trains improvisers to relate comfortably and good-naturedly to audiences faster than anything else Johnstone dreamed up, fixing the habit of talking to side walls, to the gap between stalls and dress-circle, or to an imaginary audience floating a few feet overhead.
**Rules:** 1. The speaker addresses the seated group, each member of which raises a hand and then begins to lower it slowly until the speaker makes genuine eye contact, at which point the hand returns to its first position and starts descending again. 2. If a hand descends low enough to touch the thigh, its owner says, 'Beep! Beep!' until genuine eye contact is made. 3. The speaker's task is to prevent or minimize these interruptions. 4. Open with, 'It's impossible to win this game, because the "audience" has such a great desire to say, "Beep! Beep!" that they'll speed up!', which gets everyone laughing, gives the spectators 'permission' to accelerate, and reassures the speaker of not being singled out as a 'loser'. 5. Turn to the group: 'If you feel "seen", please return your hands to their starting position and begin lowering them again.' 6. For nervous speakers who can't talk coherently at first, offer crutches: 'Just concentrate on the eye contacts!', or 'Count to a hundred out loud,' or 'Describe our physical appearance.' 7. After several speakers, raise the cheating issue: speakers complain that hands kept lowering despite eye contact, and the answer is that looking into their eyes isn't enough, they had no experience of 'being seen', you have to let them know that you 'saw' them (which is why Groucho Marx gave himself big, thick eyebrows and learned to waggle them up and down so quickly).
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Many speakers make themselves look 'dead', as if a formal photograph was being taken, but suppressing the beep-beeps makes them more expressive: they have to smile, or tilt their heads, or waggle their eyebrows, which makes them seem 'nicer' and friendlier and more alive. Groups who teach impro to managers of oil companies say the Beep-Beep Game is one of the quickest ways to turn corporate 'zombies' into human beings, and a few minutes' training can give permanent results.

## 9. Addressing an Audience
**Players:** Solo speaker
**Setup:** A theatre with stalls, dress-circle and upper-circle (Johnstone's example is the Royal Court in Sloane Square, London, where in his day the lights were dazzling so you only saw the first few rows)
**Goal:** Makes the whole audience experience themselves as 'being seen', so they warm to the speaker and feel in 'safe hands'.
**Rules:** 1. Divide the front stalls into sections one, two, three and four, and the rear stalls into sections five, six and seven. 2. Slice the dress-circle into sections eight, nine and ten, and the upper-circle into sections eleven, twelve and thirteen. 3. Stand downstage and, as you speak to the audience, let your attention switch unpredictably from section to section, giving a recognition signal each time. 4. Run through sequences like One, Three, Eight, Thirteen, Nine, Four, Ten, Two, Eleven, Five, Twelve, Six and Seven so that no one can ever guess which part of the house you will address next.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** This scales the Beep-Beep recognition signals to a full house: the Elizabethans took pleasure in speaking directly to the audience, and performers unblocked by architecture (Ken Dodd, Dame Edna) prove the problem lies in the eye-slide, not the proscenium arch.
