## 1. Gibberish Cards
**Players:** Any number working singly then in pairs
**Setup:** Gibberish sentences written along the narrow ends of playing cards to serve as scripts
**Goal:** Fluent gibberish with full vowel and consonant range, no prejudging of sounds, no 'Oggy Boggy Soggy Doggie Yoggy'
**Rules:** 1. Hand out cards and say 'Use these cards as your script.' Reading means no one can be wrong, so students who suffered agonies of embarrassment become instantly proficient. 2. Watch your partner to discover whether you gave a compliment, an insult, or a proposal of marriage. Meaning is established by the recipient, so the emphasis falls on reacting rather than acting.
**Variations:** For over-fluent players (tobacco auctioneer pace, worshippers speaking in tongues) ask them to repeat some of the phrases their partner says, sometimes be at a loss for words, and adapt their gibberish to match the partner's so both seem to be speaking the same language. Later add national flavors: Italian, German, English, Canadian, Deep South gibberish.
**Coaching:** Strasberg urged making gibberish understood. Johnstone replaces this with watching the partner. Toddlers love speaking in tongues and make safe practice partners.

## 2. A Class in Gibberish
**Players:** Pairs rotating through class
**Setup:** One gibberish card per player initially, two later
**Goal:** Ingest a lifelong gibberish vocabulary with minimal stress
**Rules:** 1. One improviser sits waiting, another enters, each holding one card: they meet, speak their sentence, and leave together. 2. Then allow two cards each. His example: [Entering] 'Mahkram Artep?' [Standing up] 'Ena hej!' [They're leaving together] 'Resarf nok!' [Nodding head] 'Reksew!' 3. Encourage a slight foreign accent. 4. When skilled, move to longer scenes in gibberish, then wean off cards by suggesting they begin adding gibberish sentences of their own.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Ask why some interactions amuse and others don't. Students adopting stupid walks and comical gestures evoke minimal response, whereas quite normal players get laughs. Said to a dull improviser 'Eid leakim!' has no effect: a brilliant one looks surprised, interested, angry, shocked, embarrassed. We laugh from delight in seeing things happen, especially a drop in status. At least one player must be altered by the other, since altering yourself as you speak has little effect.

## 3. Alternating Gibberish and English
**Players:** Two or more
**Setup:** None beyond the switch command
**Goal:** Transfer the spatial and vocal confidence characteristic of good gibberish into normal improvisations
**Rules:** 1. Version One: players switch from Gibberish to English and back on command, also runs with Party Endowments. 2. Version Two: A starts speaking gibberish while B speaks English, and any player may switch at any time, forcing the partner to switch too. His example: 'Nots ruh tyr rej?' / 'He's with a patient.' / 'Onito tenileh!' / [Switch] 'Tenileh?' / 'Yes, it's going to be twins.' / 'Adaran!' / [Switch] 'Renk luaf semaj!' / 'Oh dear! Should I call the nurse?' 3. Some changes from English to gibberish and back should occur mid-sentence.
**Variations:** Try with Party Endowments (p. 233)
**Coaching:** Mid-sentence switching is what pushes the confidence across into ordinary play.

## 4. Gibberish Shadows
**Players:** Four: two beginners play, two experienced shadows
**Setup:** Each beginner gets an experienced shadow staying close
**Goal:** Beginners play a full scene without being responsible for the sounds they make
**Rules:** 1. Two beginners play a scene normally. 2. Each has an experienced shadow who stays close and feeds them gibberish sentences throughout.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** None given

## 5. Jokes in Gibberish
**Players:** Groups of four or five
**Setup:** Swap jokes in gibberish
**Goal:** Timing learned from listeners: when to hold back, when to release
**Rules:** 1. Beginners start by translating existing jokes. 2. Then forget translation: 'Watch the onlookers for clues as to when to hold back, and when to release.' 3. The build-up to the climax can only be done by observing the listeners' reactions, correcting the common fault of continuing past the point where listeners expect the punchline.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Introduce as 'advanced' or your students may get discouraged.

## 6. Reality Testing
**Players:** Solo students plus unwitting public
**Setup:** A dirty piece of paper with a fake address
**Goal:** Authentic gibberish generated by desperation not to be rumbled
**Rules:** 1. Students dine out pretending to speak no English, which makes their gibberish very authentic because they were desperate not to be rumbled. 2. Or send them out clutching a dirty paper with a fake address, spouting gibberish to passers-by who always send them off in some definite direction.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Anecdotal RADA assignment from Gower Street days, apologies owed to Dillon's bookshop.

## 7. Guess the Situation (Gibberish)
**Players:** Threes: one guesser, two scene players, playable en masse
**Setup:** Guesser sent out of earshot while the class agrees a situation
**Goal:** Keep actors in the present moment, remove lingering gibberish anxieties, show players how misleading they are
**Rules:** 1. Agree a situation, e.g. guesser Roger is a brain surgeon and Sarah brings her son Tony to his clinic. 2. Guesser returns and is told he plays a scene in English while Sarah and Tony speak gibberish, treats them as if he understands everything, then afterwards reports who he was, who the others were, where the scene took place. 3. Scene players communicate purely through behaviour while the guesser improvises aloud and guesses: Sarah implores on her knees, Roger guesses executions and galleys, Tony points at his head, Sarah mimes chipping a hole in Tony's skull, Roger condemns adulterers, Sarah kisses Tony. 4. Stop the scene, forbid anyone to give the secret away, ask the guesser what he understood. 5. Replay with two other students profiting from the errors just seen: in the dog version (Olive is the dog who messed David's white carpet, Pat is David's sister) the new players whistle her in and press her down onto all fours until she barks and everyone cheers. 6. Divide the class into threes and play Guess the Situation en masse, encouraging fun situations such as the family returned from Mum's funeral where the guesser is Mum, i.e. Mum is a ghost.
**Variations:** Choose situations that will be fun
**Coaching:** Excellent for increasing beginner confidence. Even beginners don't mind failing at it because it is so entertaining. Blunder after blunder sweeps remaining qualms aside through sheer need to communicate.

## 8. Establishing Status Signals
**Players:** Any number, taught by lecture-demonstration
**Setup:** No props
**Goal:** Conscious control of dominance signals feeding every Status Game
**Rules:** 1. Hold eye contact to dominate, break it and take a quick glance back when feeling submissive. 2. Hands near the head signal low status: touch your mouth while looking at someone and you feel hesitant and are perceived as lowering your status. A cigarette moved towards the mouth conceals a gesture of submission, which is why smoking is cool. Touching someone's face or head raises your status if accepted, hence doing it to children and the curious behaviour of barbers. 3. Other things equal, highest status uses the most space and is the most relaxed except when challenged. Folded arms, stiff symmetrical body reads as eager to be high status yet fails against someone lying on a sofa, legs lolling apart, fearless of attack. 4. We placate higher-status people by ruining posture and constricting the voice. 5. Try-it demonstrations: say Clint Eastwood's 'Make my day' while wagging your head and notice how ineffective it becomes, and drop instantly in status by standing toes together with heels out.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Most of us are better at high or better at low, both defensive: high says 'Keep away, I bite', low says 'I'm not worth biting'. Bad drama schools give high roles to aristocratic students and subservient roles to low-status students: Hugh Crutwell at RADA did the opposite. Woody Allen raises status in order to demolish it, the gold watch inherited from the grandfather who sold it to him on his deathbed.

## 9. Circular Status
**Players:** Three or more standing in a circle
**Setup:** Circle formation
**Goal:** Practise playing high status to one person while playing low to another
**Rules:** 1. Stand players in a circle and say 'The player to your left will dominate you, and the player on your right will submit to you', producing for example John above Ron above Terra above John. 2. Play scenes inside the loop.
**Variations:** Ghost Game dialogue (p. 181) cited as an instance of the same skill
**Coaching:** Such scenes seldom get anywhere but practising the double orientation is worthwhile.

## 10. Status Parties
**Players:** Whole class divided into Group A and Group B
**Setup:** Party space, no special props
**Goal:** Split the group into high-status and low-status players and demonstrate that applying one technique makes the others lock on automatically
**Rules:** 1. Run a plain party: 'Mingle! Meet old friends. Ask where the food is. Exchange gossip. Find the hostess.' Stop after perhaps thirty seconds because anxiety makes players adopt each other's tempo and use of space. 2. Restart with contrasting assignments from his signal menus: blinking (As blink more often than usual, Bs hardly ever, opening and closing eyes slowly if they must, blinkers shortening the backs of their necks and becoming isolated, non-blinkers loud and confident, trying to dominate each other), speech (As use long grammatically complete sentences, Bs fragmented sentences and out of breath: 'Ah, Jeremy, I thought I might meet you here.' / '[Gasp] Mary . . . er . . . Well, I . . . yes, what a surprise . . .' then play the mirror version with Bs gasping through 'Rather a . . . er . . . [Gasp] good party'), heads (As keep heads absolutely still during each phrase and never touch their heads, Bs move heads normally and touch their own heads and faces), tempo (As slower and smoother than usual, delaying before replying including a physical delay, don't flinch, Bs making small unnecessary movements and responding instantly), voice and feet (As open each sentence with a long thoughtful 'errrrr' and rotate one foot slightly outward, Bs respond with an immediate tiny 'er' and turn one foot inward). 3. Have everyone try both poles.
**Variations:** Status Transitions continues directly from this game
**Coaching:** Few students enjoy both experiences equally because they have a preferred status, but require both. Non-blinkers tend to hold eye contact while blinkers keep looking away and glancing back as if power streaming from the Bs is too powerful to confront.

## 11. Two-Person Status Games
**Players:** Two plus watching class
**Setup:** Short scenes, furniture helps
**Goal:** Sustain each status configuration and learn gradual reversal
**Rules:** 1. Both play low: '[Nervous laugh] I was so . . . er . . . grateful for your . . . er . . . invitation.' / 'Oh, please . . . I never thought . . . I mean . . . I'm so . . . so proud that you came.' / 'Er . . . no one invites me anywhere, actually.' Note that low-status players are hesitant to contradict people, like the editor in Evelyn Waugh's Scoop who said 'Up to a point, Lord Cooper' when he meant No. 2. One plays high, one low: 'So this is where you live, Cedric! Rather squalid!' / 'I'll clear a space on the bed for you, sir. I'm so sorry!' / 'Bit dark in here! If you could wash the windows, we might be able to see the view.' / 'That . . . er . . . that is the view. It's a cement wall.' / 'Well, I'd have that torn down for starters . . .' 3. Both play high: 'Shoes off, Paul. Can't have you trampling dog-shit everywhere.' / 'So this is where you live? It can't be pleasant sharing that toilet on the landing.' / 'It's just a pied-a-terre. I spend most of my time abroad, actually.' / 'Come hunting on the estate this weekend. We've a deer park full of animals all trained to stand sideways so you can get a good shot at them.' 4. Start one high one low and reverse gradually during the scene: tenant avoiding the landlord, visitor offering to buy a painting, smearing the painted clouds, 'My goodness! But these clouds are so real I could reach out and touch them! Oh!' / 'You've . . . You've smeared it!' / 'I, I'm so sorry.' / 'Idiot!'
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Theatresports status challenges tend to disappoint because players milk it: crawling onstage or pretending to pick noses for low, claiming to be millionaires or rock stars for high. Ideally status permeates all scenes, a good situation being one that makes status very clear and perhaps reverses it. Unless players are experts, explicit status challenges create inferior work.

## 12. Status Transitions
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** Chairs and a sofa
**Goal:** Justified, gradual reversal of physical status positions
**Rules:** 1. Party form: replay status parties but each group gradually changes to the other group's behaviour, starters high ending low and vice versa. 2. Chair form: student sits on a chair, feet flat, toes touching, heels apart, one hand touching her mouth. Partner towers over her perhaps with one foot on an adjacent chair. Most students resist the dominant position, even leaning back, so tell them to point at their partners and yell something like 'Why did you do it?' or 'Don't you ever disobey me again': the body shifts forward into an aggressive pose and space pours over the submissive victim. 3. Play a scene ending when each has achieved the other's starting position, chairs interchangeable. Coach calls 'Keep altering! Don't get stuck! Janet, open your legs a little! Bill, touch your nose for a split second!' 4. Sofa form: one sprawls on the sofa in a high-status way, legs apart, the other kneels with head towards them and forehead touching the floor. Scenes end when they have swapped positions.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Players must find justifications for the changes.

## 13. The Rejection Game
**Players:** Groups of four or five
**Setup:** A sociable scene with real furniture: couple who moved in upstairs invited down for coffee, or four friends coming into a lounge bar. Sofa, chairs and a low coffee table help because people feel safer on a sofa than at the edge of the group
**Goal:** Toughen improvisers and make them genuinely friendly, dissolving the secret caution that isolates actors and makes movies look unreal
**Rules:** 1. Instruction to each: 'When the scene ends one of you has to be rejected by the other three, it must absolutely not be you!' This causes instant panic because the rejected person really loses, so frame it: 'No one will be forced to play this game. But if you're ejected, we can tell you why, and this will improve your ability to stay in a group,' or 'It's really a friendship game, since if you have a friend in the group, you're safe, and the two of you can eject someone else.' 2. Play, expecting minute after minute with nobody rejected: the moment anyone becomes vulnerable the others rescue them with eye contact and attention. 3. Coach demonstrations: tell a player feeling vulnerable to take his chair and move to the other side of the group to say something to another player, tying him to one person and isolating someone else, or turn someone into a ghost: 'Keep talking together, but when John says something pay not the slightest attention, so that he feels as if he doesn't exist.' Intervene if someone rescues the ghost with eye contact. 4. Valid endings: three exit leaving one onstage, one driven away, one clearly isolated and out of the group even though still physically there. 5. Strengthening techniques: talk to an individual, general remarks weaken your position. Don't control the group, taking charge and bossing isolates you. Never cross your legs away from the group. Don't fold arms or clutch the furniture or yourself. Don't pull feet under the chair. Open the body so your space flows into other people's. Find the most sympathetic person and try to link with them. And you aren't rejected until you accept rejection: stay calm and unperturbed, bide your time until you see a chance to link to somebody.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Especially scary to pubescent people because rejection is fiercest then. After a few rounds anxiety is swept away by roars of laughter and the improvisation alters: players were only pretending to be friendly before, now they genuinely are.

## 14. Being the Same Status
**Players:** Two plus observing class
**Setup:** Scene space
**Goal:** Moment-by-moment mutual observation, probably the most important Status Game
**Rules:** 1. Two players attempt to make the status gap as small as possible through a scene. 2. Coach interrupts occasionally asking the class 'Who is higher?' or 'Who is lower?' 3. Divided opinions mean success: congratulate the players and continue. If the class agrees Frank is higher than Tony, ask 'Which of them is wrong?', hoping to be told it is a silly question since either can restore the balance. 4. Once players discover mirroring solves it, place them in positions that don't allow mirroring, e.g. one sitting on the floor leaning back against the sofa the other is sitting on, positions you see at informal gatherings.
**Variations:** Anti-mirroring placements as above
**Coaching:** People are hardly ever equal in status except when forced into uniform and drilled, chorus lines and troops, so minimizing the gap is excellent training.

## 15. Fight for Your Number
**Players:** Three, or four, or three choosing between one and two only
**Setup:** Secret numbers, family casting: Dad, Mum and Mum's sister, or two teenagers and Gran
**Goal:** Model interactions between strangers and families, force status battles
**Rules:** 1. Three students each secretly choose a number one to three: Ones most dominant, Twos less dominant, Threes least dominant. 2. Cast and play scenes in which each tries to achieve their secret number. 3. Ones should dominate everyone but prefer dominating a Two, battling other Ones. Twos need Threes to lord it over and Ones to look up to. Threes look up to everybody but long for a Two to relate to. 4. Expect sons elevated unwillingly to Number One by Mum, or three sisters all trying to out-submit each other.
**Variations:** Four-person group choosing one to three, or three-person group choosing either one or two, guaranteeing at least two players fighting for the same number
**Coaching:** Some Threes become so submissive they look as if they wandered in from another pecking order: tell them not to behave like Eights.

## 16. Self Up/Partner Down
**Players:** Two or more in conversation
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Alternation strategies adding transitions, seeming more creative
**Rules:** 1. Left alone students prefer working on their own status or on the partner's: alternate deliberately. 2. Self Up/Partner Down example: 'You're not handing in written homework are you? Why don't you use a computer. Look at my essay! You'd swear it was printed professionally. I shouldn't think your grubby little paragraph is going to impress anybody . . .' 3. Self Down/Partner Up example: 'You've got such a wonderful memory. I'll forget my own name one of these days. I'm boring you, aren't I? I don't have the gift of the gab like you!'
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Alternating adds transitions and makes you seem more creative.

## 17. Playing the Wrong Status
**Players:** Two
**Setup:** Relationship implying a large gap: teacher and child, mugger and victim, brain surgeon and patient
**Goal:** Comedy and insight from inverted interactive status
**Rules:** 1. Choose the gap relationship, then play the opposite status. 2. Low-status mugger, high-status victim: 'Excuse me, miss. Er . . . Miss! Er . . .' / 'What are you muttering about?' / 'I'm a mugger! I . . . er . . . I mug people!' / 'I've read about this sort of thing . . .' / 'Give me your money! Er . . . hand it over!' / 'It's Ryan, isn't it? So this is how you show your gratitude for all those hours when I tried to teach you geography! Ungrateful boy!' / 'Auggghh!! My arm! It's broken!' / 'Just dislocated! Lie still, I'll fix it for you!' 3. Or pit a low-status hijacker against a high-status pilot: 'You want me to fly where?' / 'I'm sorry it . . . it's either Ohio or Iowa, I get them confused.' / 'You've no idea how to fire that gun, have you?' / 'Yes I do! And I'm prepared to use it!' / 'If you pull the trigger a flag will pop out. They sell them in the airport shop.'
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Peter Cook played a high-status beginner to Dudley Moore's low-status Welsh piano teacher: 'This note is A, and this is B and so on, up to G, and then we start again with A.' / 'Oh, I don't think that will do at all. We'll refer to this note on the far left as Number One . . .' / '[Gulp] Ah, well, perhaps you'd be so good as to press number twenty-two then . . .'

## 18. Passengers
**Players:** Three
**Setup:** Neutral third character added to any status scene
**Goal:** Complicate and stabilize status transactions
**Rules:** 1. Take a two-person status premise, e.g. a high-status sister haranguing her low-status brother for not cleaning the apartment. 2. Add a passenger, here a woman he picked up in a bar, who stays neutral and tries to remain uninvolved.
**Variations:** King Game Passenger Version uses the master's lover, masseur, or language teacher
**Coaching:** None given

## 19. Low-status Trick Presentation
**Players:** Two alternating entrances, rest of class cheering, optional pianist
**Setup:** A flat either side of the acting area, pianist with loud circus music optional
**Goal:** Train presentation stripped of trick content: the trick is less important than the presentation
**Rules:** 1. Tony enters from behind one flat, runs centre-stage, says 'Trick', leaves as Petra enters from the other side doing the same. Time it so there's no dead stage: overlap. 2. Round two: bow before and after you say 'Trick!' Class urged to cheer, applaud and shout 'Bravo!' throughout. 3. Run on with small steps demonstrating eagerness, and be so happy it's obvious you'd never dream of challenging their superiority. See them and bow, see them and say Trick, see them and bow again, and see them before leaving. 4. If the audience has no sensation of being seen, first play the Beep-Beep Game (p. 260), since being seen allows friendship signals and being pumped with inner delight at how wonderful the audience is. 5. With pianist: after the first bow raise a hand to stop the music. Pianist gives a drum roll while you prepare, rolling up sleeves, exercising fingers, taking deep breaths, conveying that you're about to attempt something very special and difficult, looking slightly worried about your chances of success. 6. Make them wait, absolutely, but not because you're high status: you're making them wait because the trick is so difficult and you want to do it well for them. Then say 'Trick', make a happy gesture of release, smile so we know the trick is over, pianist glissandoing back into circus music. 7. Wait until the split second after the drum-roll before deciding whether to do Johnstone's thumb trick or something spontaneous: stand on one leg, put your tongue out twisting an ear. Any trick passes as long as the build-up is well done. 8. Thumb trick: clasp your fist with the thumb inside, keep it still, hit it from underneath with your other fist letting the thumb shoot upright as if the blow created the effect. Perform it in front of the chest or to the side if costume jangles, never in front of the face where it confuses the image.
**Variations:** Give a simple real trick once the presentation is achieved
**Coaching:** Richardson Morgan mimed unsheathing a sword each entrance doing tricks like cutting his hand off, vanishing it into his sleeve, slicing a fly into eight pieces, knighting an audience member, swallowing the sword and extracting it from below: minutes passed with escalating ecstasy built on enthusiasm and understanding that the actual trick was of no importance. Correct presentation makes our cheering effortless, screwing it up forces us to shout Bravo. Now taught in clown schools with exactly the rules devised in the sixties.

## 20. Kinetic Dance Freeze Demonstration
**Players:** Whole class with two seated volunteers frozen
**Setup:** Chairs
**Goal:** Develop conscious awareness of the kinetic dance, the synchronized connectivities that restore agreed status
**Rules:** 1. Background theory: touch your face for a moment and partners will almost certainly move to restore agreed status, rotating a foot inward, moving a thumb closer to fingers, or touching their own face, adaptations becoming obvious whenever someone enters or leaves a group. 2. Ask the class to freeze and maintain the direction their eyes look. 3. Pick two students sitting next to each other, keep them frozen while the rest walk to the opposite wall. The couple will look emotional: reluctant to make up after a quarrel, or very lonely, often one angry and one repentant, because they shared their space with people now gone. 4. Reverse the message by lifting one of them complete with chair and placing them on the opposite side: slight hostility flips to friendliness. 5. Say 'Okay, you can move', turn away thanking them, then ask 'What's happening now?': the frozen pair makes instant, effortless, unconscious adjustments until finding a correct relation to each other and to the group.
**Variations:** By this and similar tricks
**Coaching:** Connectivities are suppressed by grief, hostility and 'acting'. Absence of the dance tells coach and players something needs fixing. Onstage the relationship defaults to actor versus audience unless a character status relationship is achieved, at which point the dance kicks in and actors respond intuitively, no longer experiencing spectators as stern judges.

## 21. Party Endowments
**Players:** Four volunteers, preferably two men and two women
**Setup:** Party space with door, drinks, bookcase
**Goal:** Focus attention on the other players, create transitions, teach discretion
**Rules:** 1. Each secretly endows the other three with qualities: 'There are three people on the stage with you. Decide that one is stupid, and that one is smelly, and that one is attractive.' Keep it secret. Treating someone as deaf doesn't make them deaf, and disagreements about people are ordinary: we can't even agree about flavours of ice-cream. 2. Cast one player as host, three guests entering at fifteen-second intervals so we can see what's happening, door locked so the host must answer it instead of shouting Come in. 3. It's a party because parties carry an obligation to be polite: no 'You smell like a fish!', find some more civilized way to solve the problem. 4. Forbid demonstrating attitudes: the host recoiling and gasping for air at the smelly guest gets 'Don't let him know that he's smelly! Be courteous!' Maximum permitted actions: open a window or say casually 'Came straight from work, did you?' Leave to answer the phone, or make introductions to escape. Guest contemptuous toward a host endowed idiot gets 'You're endowing your host with being an idiot, but you accepted the invitation. Can't you deal with stupid people without offending them?' Blatant admiration of the sexy guest gets 'Be afraid of being rejected!' or 'Don't be thought a slut!' 5. Concealment side-coaching: 'Conceal your feelings! Move your chair just half an inch!' A smelly person endowing you with attractiveness may be hard to shake off: find something interesting on the bookcase, go admire the view. Only be rude if someone is an out-and-out pest. 6. Purpose-giving side-coaching to convert chat into purposes: introduce people, offer drinks, 'Remember the present you left in the car!', 'Have a heart-attack!', 'Start a quarrel', 'Hold a seance!', 'Offer to help! Offer to grind the coffee! Remember something important that you need to tell them!', 'Find a way to get the attractive one to a bedroom!', 'Avoid the smelly one!', 'The funny one is getting funnier!' 7. For the funny endowment interrupt cautious laughter: ask players to point at the person they find amusing, then laugh next time that person speaks even at 'It's a nice day.' Restrain laughter demonstrated to the audience. 8. After the scene ask players to point to the sexy one and the smelly one and so on: vigorous laughing affirms it was just a game.
**Variations:** Once familiar replace stupidity with being amusing, giving sexy, smelly, funny. Alternate gibberish and English. Layer into Sandwiches. Move the party to a new locale such as a cinema queue or the park.
**Coaching:** Endowment menu offered: a reputation for hitting people at parties, easily shocked, tactless, nosy, rather delicate needing cosseting, physically fit energetic muscular, rich and desirable, makes snide remarks, unimportant and irritating, deeply religious, hypnotic personality, compulsive liar. Audience comprehension is unnecessary: 'If you're doing something that they can't fathom, you'll be a lot more interesting than if you held up a sign.' The kinetic dance locks in when players endow each other with qualities and vanishes instantly when they think I'll be the sexy one.

## 22. Endow Everything
**Players:** Three or four, use three for the shifting version
**Setup:** Repeat a Party Endowments scene with the same endowments
**Goal:** Contrasting attitudes to objects and topics generating transitions and aliveness
**Rules:** 1. Replay the scene with the same person-endowments plus having an attitude to whatever else you relate to. 2. Dull route: similar attitude to everything, dirty sofa, holes in the carpet, depressing view, wallpaper needing replacement, stale biscuits. 3. Interesting route: choose contrasts: old but comfy sofa, tasteful wallpaper, coffee-table like the one you have at home, rather squalid area with magnificent view, creating a transition every time you switch attention. 4. Side-coach topics: 'Praise the movie! Change expression when ice-hockey is mentioned! Have an attitude to dogs!'
**Variations:** After practice shift attitudes to each person mid-scene: let the smelly one gradually become attractive, the boring one saintly. Move the party to a new locale: cinema queue, the park.
**Coaching:** Use three players, four is too complex.

## 23. Sandwiches
**Players:** Four standing in a circle
**Setup:** Party Endowments skills already rehearsed
**Goal:** Stack multiple simultaneous games into one scene
**Rules:** 1. Standing in a circle announce: 'Point to the person on your left. This person is higher status than you. Now point to the person on your right: this person is lower status than you. The remaining person is the same status as you.' 2. Play a scene using the same endowments as before plus these status endowments, six qualities per player, three already practised. 3. Interesting combinations arise: endowing your unusually attractive partner with low status, a smelly player with high status. 4. Deeper layers: attitude to every topic brought up, moving from one emotional state to another, sustaining a body-image such as heavy sustained and indirect from Fast-Food Laban, pursuing a Stanislavskian objective.
**Variations:** Combine Fast-Food Stanislavsky with Fast-Food Laban (pp. 283-301), or with Fight for Your Number
**Coaching:** Such sandwiches sound insanely complicated yet we achieve them effortlessly in life.

## 24. The King Game
**Players:** One master, successive servants, one timekeeper
**Setup:** Master on a throne or comfortably arranged on a sofa with cushions, grapes and other comforts
**Goal:** Explore maximum-gap status transactions, compete for longest servant survival, produce exquisitely attentive play
**Rules:** 1. Rule announced to the master: if anything irritates you no matter how minor, snap your fingers and your servant commits suicide, to be instantly replaced ad infinitum. 2. Appoint a timekeeper: 'We'll call fifteen seconds good! And twenty seconds excellent! Anyone like to try for twenty-five?' This gives masters permission to be severe. 3. Masters kill on impulse. Servants strive to make being onstage a pleasure. 4. Interrupt forgiving masters and point out the moments of irritation: 'You wanted to kill him after three seconds because his demeanour challenged you . . . Then you wanted to kill him because he cringed . . . because he asked you what you wanted . . . because he passed right in front of you on the way to the drinks cabinet . . . because he took a position behind you that made you feel uncomfortable. How will our servants learn if you're so forgiving?' Read the master's body: biting lip and frowning is unhappiness so kill, shirting about on the throne means snap, elbow shoved into your face means don't give her a chance, she's been pestering you from the moment she stepped onstage. 5. Named servant crimes: big steps and waving arms like a scullery-maid brought in while the regular servant is ill, standing over the master so uncomfortably that he crosses his leg away, the unsuccessful James Bond move, holding eye contact forcing the master to look away, asking if there is anything you want which forces a decision, since servants are paid to take trivial decisions away from masters. 6. Servant advice list: enter with a purpose. Never say 'Is there anything you need, ma'am?' Be physically and vocally discreet. Convey that the space belongs to the master and intrude only when necessary. Don't look servile or as if waiting to be punished. Don't out-stare, meaning out-status, the master. Don't leave awkward pauses. Giving a newspaper, serving coffee or breakfast may help but survival needs something of dramatic interest happening. Winning strategies: purposeful business like opening curtains or reporting an assassination plot, or discovering the jester role as entertainer or confidant, the clown in King Lear, John Brown tipping with Queen Victoria at Balmoral.
**Variants:** Die at the second finger-snap or third, saved until interest needs increasing: servants shocked by the first snap continue in exactly the same suicidal way. Pecking-order Version: once a servant survives indefinitely call in an under-servant killable by either servant or master, timing under-servants too. Degenerate Version where the master tells servants what to do and kills poor performance is pointless. Passenger Version: add the master's lover, masseur, or language teacher.
**Coaching:** Ask servants why they were killed: usually no idea, and masters often don't know either. Frame effort kindly: palace servants take months or years before serving the king at banquet, this takes only an hour or so. The real secret is to involve the master in an interesting situation, not just to serve: offering mimed drinks or foot-massages helps but drama sustains survival.

## 25. Master-Servant
**Players:** Two or more
**Setup:** Formal service setting, tea and cucumber sandwiches
**Goal:** Express parent-child material in disguised form so players work out personal traumas without realizing it, excellent with children
**Rules:** 1. Create a platform by having the servant serve the master formally. 2. Break the routine of serving: 'Thank-you, Perkins.' / 'Might I have a word with you, sir?' / 'Fire away!' / 'I left Sammy's bowl on the record-player at speed thirty-three and a third, and when I came back you had him on forty-five.' / 'I was just trying to build up his muscle.' / 'Build up his muscle? The water was practically boiling.' / 'You exaggerate, Perkins!' / 'You just wait till I run your bath for you!' 3. Present spectators hoping personal material cracks the formality: 'Was that my daughter I heard in your room last evening, Perkins?' or 'Have you been reading my private diary, sir?' or 'Why didn't you tell me I was your son?' 4. Core principle: it's a mistake to assume master equals high status and servant low, confusing social with interactive status. The servant can beat the master while still looking like the servant: whatever status you play, always treat the space, the territory, as belonging to the master.
**Variations:** Blind Offer Version: the master snaps his or her fingers, gives no verbal orders, and the servant always knows what to do, the response always right, building a scene this way. Piggy-Back: masters use servants as horses, entering and leaving riding piggy-back: a good master and good servant play a scene ending in a gallop off to the park, a bad pair does the same thing, both pairs meet at the park and quarrel, one master challenges the other to a joust using boxing gloves on poles, perhaps injury or love, or quarrels without jousting and two further separate Master-Servant scenes before meeting again to fight the duel. Give the masters balloons to beat their servants with (p. 259).
**Coaching:** Piggy-back: take care not to injure anyone.

## 26. Slow-motion Commentary
**Players:** One mover, one commentator, optionally a colour-person
**Setup:** Stage space, sports-commentary frame
**Goal:** Variety, present-moment bodily attention, hindering players from searching ahead for good ideas
**Rules:** 1. Put one player onstage moving in extreme slow-motion, another commentates on the spectacle. In all slow-motion work the body's centre of gravity must stay in motion no matter how minutely: even one finger moving less than an inch every five seconds needs connection to the body's centre, making movements integrated rather than robotic parts moving one at a time. 2. The commentator mixes modes: sometimes adding trivia, sometimes going mad with excitement, sometimes deploring imaginary infractions, sometimes falling silent as if nothing could be added, letting the event speak for itself. 3. Imaginary trivia cards for competitors: 'Ron Harris is wearing the red jock-strap that has become his most famous distinguishing mark,' or 'Plucky little Audrey Swinburne's father took time off from the local glue factory to supervise her training . . . her swimsuit was knitted by her eighty-two-year-old grandmother.' 4. Colour-person usually plays an expert or the athlete's trainer. Door-entry example: 'Oh . . . and I think something's happening. I can see a slit of light around the edge of the door . . . Difficult to be sure as the knob is entirely circular. What do you think, Kevin?' / 'I think it must be turning, Monty, otherwise the Judges would have counted this competitor out by now.' 5. Commentary can direct the player: 'I don't think he's set himself up properly for this move . . . He's about to give himself a most tremendous crack on the head in about three minutes' time . . .' possibly closing 'Rather a slow start at the International Entering A Room competition here at Chutney. And now it's time for these important messages . . .' 6. Commentator guidelines: allow the event to speak for itself. Get excited and build climaxes even though no climax exists. Explain finer points of the game, infractions and so on. Supply background information on characters or things peripheral to the event. His full Frank Quimby Barnsley Toad-Swallowing commentary demonstrates mode-switching with long pauses, judge disputes over freezing with terror, crowd fighting, trainer time-outs overruled, and a baulk predicted minutes early, all while Frank inclines his head one inch baring his teeth slightly.
**Variations:** Two or more players moving in slow-motion. Commentator supplies his own colour-person and so on using different voices. Wider Applications: lay commentary onto normal-speed scenes, e.g. two players trying to keep status as close as possible: 'She's flashing her top teeth at him, Tom.' / 'Yes, she's definitely lowering her status by that move. He'll have to respond to that if he wants to keep the balance. Yes, he's putting his hand to his face.' / 'Her blink-rate has increased, Tom, and she's a little breathless when she speaks. He'll have to compensate.' Such commentary can side-coach in ways too intrusive otherwise: 'that's the third idea he's killed. This scene is becoming weighed down by a relentless negativity. What do you think, Nigel?'
**Coaching:** Expect to shout 'Slow down! Do less! Move imperceptibly!' Whenever the commentator hints something is imminent players speed up unless restrained: the elegant performer maintains exactly the same pace, a refusal to accelerate that can be magical. Reject the New Zealand game-sheet description of commentators talking as quickly as possible: real-life commentators would be fired for such intrusion. Players must allow gaps so comments can be inserted, calming rattled beginners, but commentators must be experts so practise the skills in class rather than public. Benchmark: Ben Benison and Rick Morgan mimed a table-setting at the Mercury Theatre Notting Hill in 1969 lasting eight timed minutes with no music and no commentary, audience enthralled.

## 27. Verse
**Players:** Two or more, spectators may prompt rhymes
**Setup:** Spoken couplets
**Goal:** Force abandonment of control of the future, get sucked into adventures beyond conscious control
**Rules:** 1. Say any line that comes to you and trust that either you or your partner finds a second line, or someone prompts you: spectators laugh when a rhyme is bad, applaud an apt one. 2. Accept whatever the mind dredges up. His chain: 'Well, here I am on holiday.' / 'I think I'll row across the bay!' Rowing may have been the last thing in your mind, but go with it. / 'Here's what I need, a little boat.' The next line eludes you while mentally cycling stoat, cantaloupe and grope, someone shouts Float: 'I think I'll see if it will float!' / 'Oh dear, it seems the boat is sinking, / I cannot swim' saved by someone calling 'And I've been drinking!' then 'A mermaid sees my horrid plight, / And keeps me in her bed all night': launched unintentionally into Little Mermaid. 3. Spectators see you allowing verse to control you and admire your courage launching onto a sea of couplets.
**Variations:** Popular challenges and forfeits in Micetro and Gorilla Theatre: best scene in verse, limerick based on an audience member's name, best epic poem lasting until the audience begs for mercy. Verse spoken or sung.
**Coaching:** We live in a verseless culture compared with Elizabethan England or Sei Shonagon's Japan: ask students to learn limericks so they at least know the form, then offer travesties before originals: 'Forgive me please, you corpse with bleeding wounds / That I am courteous to your murderers' beside Mark Antony's 'Oh pardon me thou bleeding piece of earth, / That I am meek and gentle with these butchers.' Or 'Heavenly powers, come and keep us safe!' beside Hamlet's 'Angels and Ministers of Grace, defend us!' Even the dullest student likely feels the increase of power, some get hooked.

## 28. The Klutz
**Players:** One solo, or improviser with audience volunteer
**Setup:** Stage space
**Goal:** Laughable repeated self-injury
**Rules:** 1. Pretend to hurt yourself, recover gradually, then hurt yourself again. 2. Sound matters more than physicality: scream and say 'I can't move!' and audiences see cramp or a slipped disc although you've done nothing physical. 3. Pain must be exaggerated to demonstrate it isn't real or the laughter stops immediately, unless they hate you. 4. Follow W. C. Fields: hide the pain with embarrassment, and the more you do that the better they like it. 5. Distinguish fast-pain, a jab with a pin, from slow-pain, a crushed foot. Suggested injuries unlikely to cause serious harm: bite tongue, be stung by an insect, slap leg harder than intended, electric shock, poke self in eye, elbow slipping off table so the head your hand was supporting falls to the table making the bang with the other hand, catching one foot behind the heel of the other while walking then trying not to trip, hand stuck in jug or vase, hurting a tooth biting something, bumping into furniture.
**Variations:** Theatre Machine travelling version played with an audience volunteer: improviser ends crippled but eager to interact. Derek Flores park-bench scene: audience volunteer asked to use the 'bad things happen to everyone I make friends with' tilt, Flores injuring himself again and again while persuading her it was coincidence, giving the impression of a man so driven by carnal desire he'd walk through fire.
**Coaching:** Knock-about comedians compare bruises but comedy lies in what happens after the pain. Taller players suffer greater bodily strain, shorter ones can learn spectacular falls: study tumbling with a good trainer.

## 29. Being Concussed
**Players:** Two, e.g. servant and master, or balloon games
**Setup:** Falls and impacts
**Goal:** Confusion releasing laughter more than impact
**Rules:** 1. A servant takes the master's hand and throws himself into a forward roll intending to look thrown by the master: unless he pretends to be disorientated it seems like a gymnastic trick. 2. Hit by a balloon and falling as if hit by an iron bar: look in the wrong direction as you scramble to your feet. The bafflement and double-takes release laughter more than the blow or the crash. 3. Case study: Ben Benison hit an audience volunteer, a gymnast, who jerked into the air, rotated horizontal and crashed onto concrete: horrifying, but had he clambered up staring in all directions it would have been hysterically funny.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Childhood trampoline clowns: two were funny, the third copying them wasn't, and he was the one who never looked confused. When clowns fall or are biffed we may be laughing at their changed attitude to the space.

## 30. Staying with It (Not Advancing)
**Players:** One performer with coach, or pairs: performer plus trainer
**Setup:** A precisely defined action
**Goal:** Extemporize on whatever you're doing at the split second of command irrespective of plans, attending to the present
**Rules:** 1. Coach says 'Make the activity more interesting.' Catch evasions: feeding a dog becomes shouting for a runaway dog, reading a newspaper becomes dialling to cancel a picnic, being negative so nothing happens. Insist on staying with the activity: find the news item difficult to read and take out a magnifying glass, gasp with horror and justify it, the friend murdered in the item, add disgusted or interested sounds, read the item aloud, mime showing it to the audience. Let extra ideas float in one ear and out the other. 2. Boiled egg elaborations: mime tapping the egg with your spoon and hear it tap back, attack it with a bread-knife, pick it up and bash it on the table, attack with bread-knife and succeed, levering the flap up very slowly and suspiciously and receiving a squirt in the eye as the egg seals itself, wrestling the lid against something trying to keep it closed from inside. 3. Select details from running scenes: 'Wait! What are you doing at this exact moment?' 'We're going to put a worm on the hook!' 'That's your intention, what are you actually doing?' 'Getting the worm out of the tin.' 'See how long you can keep us interested in that activity.' They shake the tin, poke a finger in and get bitten, stick on a Band-Aid, order the worm to leave the tin, tempt it with a voluptuous finger, hypnotize it and get hypnotized instead, threaten it with a revolver. 4. Define actions broadly: 'brushing hair' could include brushing other people's hair or a giant's, 'scratching my back' includes a chair-leg or rubbing against a tree but forbids scratching someone else's back, 'swimming' includes breast-stroke, waterfall, drowning, emerging in heaven in an ocean of champagne. Waiting for a friend at a bus stop permits phoning to find out what happened: phoning a taxi is allowed if the action was defined as waiting. Brushing teeth when a tooth drops out: brush the tooth. 5. Elegant selection: notice what is already happening: breathing, growing older, touching your nose. Charlie Rivel extended preparing to sit on a chair for minutes without getting his behind onto the seat. 6. Speed training in pairs: performer defines then elaborates an action, trainer gives encouragement and stops digressions, suggesting ideas when the performer dries, sharing the trouble. Breathing sequence: breathe more fully, add a contented 'ahhhhhh' on the exhale, mime opening a window exulting in fresh air, add resistance, pant and take little gasps, suffocate, push ribs in and out manually, crawl to a table, open a drawer, seize a knife, perform a tracheotomy on yourself. 7. Universal expanders: add dialogue or change emotion. Stroking a cat: explain to the audience how it soothes you, how sensual it is, or change to rage: 'This cat is so spoiled. Who strokes me? Who gets my meals? Who lets me sleep in a patch of sunlight all day!' 8. Waiting-for-instructions expansion: phoning someone, asking the audience for advice, praying to God, consulting encyclopedias, shouting 'What am I supposed to do?', looking through scraps of paper, trying activities at random, weeping in despair, slashing wrists and having God say 'That's right, Sidney!' while screaming 'But I'm not Sidney! I'm Philip! What are Philip's instructions?' Character out of Beckett or Kafka.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Bodies are in the present, so this procedure hinders searching ahead. Held to one activity, silent-movie and cartoon ideas present themselves automatically. Command issued at the moment you drop an egg generates Keaton chains: grab, slip, dive just before it hits, knock it out the window accidentally, bird flies off with it, motorbike pursuit, truck slams into you, grab the egg hurtling through air. Make one small activity more interesting and you'll seem a natural comedian.

## 31. Advancing the Activity
**Players:** One performer plus trainer or coach
**Setup:** Follow-on drill from Staying with It
**Goal:** Expand small excursions into unanticipated new activities
**Rules:** 1. Players staying with an action inevitably take excursions: stopping a bleeding finger might include running for a first-aid box, kept short or it reads as advancing. 2. Trainer watches, then: 'What are you doing now?' 'Trying to open the egg.' 'Weren't you sighing?' 'Was I?' 'Make "sighing" more interesting.' She has advanced from failing to open the egg to sighing. 3. The new action must also be made interesting: sighing leads to suicidal despair with tearful verbalization about a life being a series of fiascos including not being able to open an egg, planning a really interesting suicide such as battering herself to death with the egg. 4. Trainer shouts 'Advance!' as she glances at it: 'What are you doing?' 'Looking at the egg.' 'Make "looking at the egg" more interesting.'
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Advances chain indefinitely, each new activity demanding its own elaboration.
