## 1. Progressive Desensitization Drill
**Players:** Whole class, teacher-led
**Setup:** None physical. Applied across a teaching sequence toward material a group avoids.
**Goal:** Coax students into dangerous areas, taboo, personal, frightening material, without panic or retreat.
**Rules:** 1. Build a hierarchy from least to most frightening, Wolpe's feather in a far country, then a stuffed bird, then a live bird in a cage. 2. Present the most harmless item first while students stay relaxed and amused. 3. Ascend one small step at a time. 4. If anyone trembles or panics the step was too steep: back off to something less scary and proceed again. 5. Sequence games so each slightly approaches the avoided area, using laughter to keep tension discharged.
**Variations:** His wish to feed beginners while they were improvising, food as relaxation anchor.
**Coaching:** Reassure and protect while coaxing. Establish craziness as a mark of sanity so students join the club instead of snapping back into numbness.

## 2. Paradoxical Practice, Rehearse the Symptom
**Players:** Whole class, pairs, or individuals
**Setup:** Identify a specific unwanted habit: blocking, killing ideas, approval-seeking.
**Goal:** Bring the fault under conscious control by performing it deliberately.
**Rules:** 1. Before teaching acceptance instruct: Kill every idea you're offered. Have them do it enthusiastically. 2. Debrief the feeling of killing an idea. 3. Return to normal playing. Students now recognize negativity when it occurs accidentally.
**Variations:** Works for any symptom: deliberate waffling, deliberate refusal, deliberate showing-off.
**Coaching:** Doing it wrong puts everyone in a good mood. Risks are non-existent: students just become more objective and better able to modify behaviour.

## 3. Tug-o'-War
**Players:** Whole class in pairs, teacher demonstration, optional one-versus-all finale
**Setup:** Open space, rope entirely mimed
**Goal:** Expose inappropriate competitiveness. On stage we swim or sink together, and losing good-naturedly puts everyone in a good mood.
**Rules:** 1. Everyone finds a partner. Mime picking up a rope, have a mimed tug-o'-war. 2. Teacher stares out the window so nobody can scan for approval. Listen: straining, gasping. 3. Stop after about thirty seconds. Nobody has lost, and some ropes have gotten longer. 4. Teacher and a hefty student do a real tug-o'-war: about two seconds. Ask why the mimed ones lasted nearly a minute with no winner. 5. Replay the mime: Do whatever you think will please your partners. If they want to lose, pull them. If they want to win, agree to be pulled. Observe the happy smile on the winners' faces. 6. Finale: a smallish student tugs against the whole class. Who would an audience want to win? The underdog jerks the rope and everyone falls into a giggling heap.
**Variations:** Warning tale from Stuttgart: two students tried to win at losing by charging forward and smashing bald heads together.
**Coaching:** Shout Give in! Lose! Be what your partner wants you to be. Unless you are willing to be changed you might as well be working alone.

## 4. Giving Presents
**Players:** Pairs, may rotate through several partners
**Setup:** Open space, mimed objects
**Goal:** Train defining and delighting in received offers. Foundation of listening.
**Rules:** Round one, giver defines: 1. Pairs mime giving presents to each other. 2. Receiver must be delighted: Oh that's just what I wanted! 3. Keep exchanging. Note identity assertion: the tough student gives a skull, a gun, or a sharpened bicycle chain; the sensitive one gives sonnets or a flower. Round two, receiver defines: 1. New partners. 2. Don't define the present you give. Define the present you receive, and make it something that you really want! Sample: I've got this for you. Oh! It's the coat I saw in the shop window. But I have a surprise for you! A mechanical bird! Does it sing? Press the button. What is it? It's so heavy! Open it and see! An inflatable Parthenon! I was saving up for one!
**Variations:** Suitable even for three-year-olds.
**Coaching:** Defining the present you receive gives the illusion of revealing less of yourself, so defences drop and presents get more varied. Defining the present you receive is the secret of being a good listener.

## 5. Was the Work Good?
**Players:** Pairs, debrief after any partnered scene
**Setup:** Post-scene question period
**Goal:** Move attention from self-evaluation to the partner's experience.
**Rules:** 1. After a paired scene ask: Was the work good? 2. Expect confusion, then clarify: Your work is good if your partner enjoyed working with you! 3. During play keep checking on your partner to make sure they are having a good time.
**Variations:** Never praise a player whose partner looked bad.
**Coaching:** If you're inept and yet everyone wants you as their partner, you'll soon be one of the very best.

## 6. Playing Tag
**Players:** Whole group
**Setup:** Large grassy area preferred
**Goal:** Restore childhood pleasure in risking capture, surface avoidance behaviour.
**Rules:** 1. Simply play tag. 2. Watch for players sprinting for the horizon to avoid being chased: trying to win by refusing to play. 3. Teach that players who risk being caught get the most pleasure. 4. If a hunter staggers toward exhaustion, enter the game yourself and allow yourself to be tagged.
**Variations:** Tag in Pairs: partners link arms and play joined; match pairs evenly. Extreme slow-motion tag: compulsive winners betray themselves by speeding up; use lapses to discuss inappropriate thinking.
**Coaching:** Good-natured students never let a hunter stagger into exhaustion.

## 7. Blame Me
**Players:** Teacher and class, triggered whenever any student fails
**Setup:** Ongoing class, any exercise
**Goal:** Remove fear of humiliation so even shy students volunteer.
**Rules:** 1. Wait until someone fails visibly. 2. Immediately take the blame aloud: It's all my fault. I gave you the wrong game. I should have rescued you sooner! I'm so stupid! 3. Remind them you are supposed to be the expert, so it follows logically that you are at fault. 4. Give students the internal script: Keith got me up here, and he wasn't any help. What an idiot! 5. Offer alternative targets: blame the situation, the oversized class, the air-conditioning noise. Never blame yourself. 6. Keep doing it until they believe you mean it.
**Variations:** Any plausible external scapegoat invented on the spot.
**Coaching:** Once students believe it, volunteers come forward knowing they will not be humiliated.

## 8. Drop Dead, Keith
**Players:** Teacher plus any player who keeps checking for approval mid-scene
**Setup:** Scene work in progress
**Goal:** Eliminate split-second eye contacts to the director for reassurance.
**Rules:** 1. Detect quick glances at you mid-scene. 2. Shout: Say, Drop dead, Keith! 3. The player is too worried about your opinion to insult you, so the eye contacts cease.
**Variations:** Redirect attention instead: run exercises in pairs with fresh partners each round.
**Coaching:** Only attempt with strong existing rapport.

## 9. Volunteers, and Failure
**Players:** Teacher plus class, two volunteers per demonstration
**Setup:** Start of any demo exercise
**Goal:** Normalize volunteering and teach that errors are the mechanism of learning.
**Rules:** 1. Call out: Two volunteers! You and you! 2. Interrogate the picked ones: What's wrong? You looked the picture of health before I dragged you out! Explain that looking sick is a pre-emptive sympathy ploy worth extra credit. 3. If nobody volunteers name the stall: They're waiting to see if I'm going to ask them to do something difficult. Advise: volunteer with a blank mind and see what happens. 4. Teach learning by falling off: stilts, bikes on grass, juggling facing a bed. 5. Tell the Gallway tennis story: the student calls where each ball lands until accuracy arrives once she stops flinching from her errors. 6. Cite Nicolaides: The sooner you make your first five thousand mistakes the sooner you will be able to correct them. 7. Push the shy: shy people should volunteer first, the longer you delay the greater the chance someone else does what you planned. 8. Frame hard games as advanced and predict failure in advance so students fail with honour.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** If you succeed brilliantly you'll depress the rest of us and we'll never get any volunteers.

## 10. When Students Fail
**Players:** Teacher plus the failing student
**Setup:** Immediately after visible struggle
**Goal:** Treat failure as survivable so tension drops instead of compounding.
**Rules:** 1. Deadpan: You're not very good at this game. It'll take you twice as long to learn it! Failure framed as ordinary. 2. If failing continues: Excellent! We've found something that's really difficult for you! 3. Or run the interrogation: How many times have you played this game? Never! Are you supposed to be good at it? Not really. So why shouldn't you screw-up? 4. Announce precise mastery time while moving on: It'll take you twenty minutes to become proficient.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** The prediction is unverifiable since you move to another game, but it implies total confidence they will succeed.

## 11. Forcing Failure
**Players:** Technicians and Judges, players in earshot
**Setup:** Theatresports rehearsals and matches
**Goal:** Build boldness in support roles, normalize error.
**Rules:** 1. Pestering speech to techies: No one's complaining about you! You're supposed to make several screw-ups per match. Theatresports is disposable theatre! 2. Techies assert themselves mid-show: door opens into a blast of organ music, stained-glass window fades in, love theme oozes in, moon appears, prop boat pushed on, lovers eloping across a lake that was never in their minds. 3. Abuse the Judges within players' earshot: Why so cautious? Get the scorecards up faster! Take risks!
**Variations:** Full unions of sound, light, action and scenography hijacking scenes into new stories.
**Coaching:** Timid technicians cannot create the magic. Abusing the Judges makes bad decisions hard for players to resent.

## 12. Be Average, Trying Demos
**Players:** Teacher plus one tense student, class watching
**Setup:** Ordinary room, a chair to look at
**Goal:** Prove that trying degrades automatic skill, license students to be average.
**Rules:** 1. Ask: Are you trying your best? Challenge it: gymnasts tried with all their might and fell off the bars. 2. Perception demo: Look at the chair. Now TRY to look at the chair. Did it help? 3. Motor demo: Touch your nose. Now do it again but TRY to touch it. 4. Rigidity demo: Try to make your arm immovable, and it becomes easy for the coach to move because half its muscles assist. 5. Poll athletes: When was your fastest time? Were you trying hardest? Expect: funny you should ask, I really had no idea how fast I was going.
**Variations:** Plank image: anyone walks a plank, but over an abyss fear glues us to it. Treat the abyss as ordinary and cross in your average manner.
**Coaching:** We only try when we don't trust the forces within us.

## 13. Avoid the Letter S
**Players:** Two or more
**Setup:** Any conversation
**Goal:** Low-difficulty warm-up that makes players attend to their own words playfully.
**Rules:** The first player to use any word containing the letter s loses. Continue talking normally.
**Variations:** See Three-word and One-word Sentences in chapter 8.
**Coaching:** Used to warm up a lunch-time show team precisely because it is easy. His players later banned e, which caused long pauses and no trust.

## 14. Yes Instead of No, Parcel Drill
**Players:** Teacher plus one cautious beginner, or pairs
**Setup:** Any simple opening offer, typically involving a parcel
**Goal:** Break the reflex of rejection. Plain acceptance reads as talent.
**Rules:** 1. Begin: Did you bring the parcel? Typical defensive reply: No. Flag it. 2. Retrain: whatever the impulse, answer yes. Did the parcel arrive? Yes, shall I open it? There seems to be something alive in it! The time-machine? It's out in the hall. They want it back by last Thursday. 3. Compare the two rounds aloud.
**Variations:** Generalize to any offer in any scene.
**Coaching:** It takes no more talent to accept the parcel than to reject it, yet the yes-sayer is instantly perceived as talented.

## 15. Disconnected Nouns Demo
**Players:** One speaker, rest observing
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Demonstrate the social-self censorship that jams the imagination.
**Rules:** 1. Shout disconnected nouns as loudly and quickly as possible: Cabbage! Match-box! Shoe-lace! Electron! Lithium! 2. Notice the stall around the fifth word when one feels too inexplicable. 3. Discuss the sensation.
**Variations:** Repeat with reassurance and laughter to extend the run.
**Coaching:** The jam proves the imagination is policed. Reframe: the imagination is a huge animal with a will of its own. Be interested in it, but accept no responsibility. You're not its keeper.
