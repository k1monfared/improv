## 1. Blind Offers
**Players:** two improvisers (in the model scene, Sir and Perkins)
**Setup:** bare stage
**Goal:** trains making offers that leave definitions open for the partner, and accepting the partner's definitions, instead of controlling the scene
**Rules:** 1. An offer is anything you say or do, and offers can be interesting, dull, controlling, or blind. 2. Make a blind offer by withholding the definition: presenting a mimed object while saying "You forgot this yesterday" is a blind offer, while "You left your hat here" is a controlling offer. 3. Model scene: "Good morning, Perkins!" "[Blind offer] We're ready for you now, sir." "[Blindish offer] And the frog?" "It's had the injection, sir." Sir snaps his fingers (a blind offer) and Perkins mimes dressing him in heavy protective clothing. Sir splutters and Perkins mimes opening the face-plate. "There's no air in here, Perkins." "I'm sorry, sir. I'll go behind the protective wall and start pumping." "Very well!" Sir clumps toward the imaginary frog. 4. Whatever your partner's blind offer implies, accept it: Perkins decided on the spur of the moment to be a lab assistant and Sir accepted him as such. There was no frog until Sir said "And the frog?", and he could have said any other noun. 5. Notice that a blind offer such as "We are ready for you now, sir" has infinite possibilities: Perkins could mime sealing a frog in a flask and Sir could say "We've got you now, Hillary, and we're going to throw you in the sea, and God help any poor soul who rescues you!", or Sir could say "Thank you, Perkins. Are the candles lit?" "Yes, sir, and the congregation has sung the first hymn." "You know, this is the first time I've ever anointed anyone!"
**Variations:** Johnstone floats a possible new game: "the first person to make a controlling offer loses"
**Coaching:** Never to know the next step but to have the courage to take it is an exhilarating way to improvise, but beginners and frightened improvisers prefer controlling offers ("I've a present for you! It's for your birthday! It's a bottle of whisky! It's your favourite!"), which alienate the partner into furious blocking ("It's not my birthday! And I never drink alcohol! Who are you, anyway? How dare you burst in here!"). The blind-offer scenes were so coherent that the Stage newspaper accused the company of presenting rehearsed scenes under the guise of improvisation.

## 2. Justify the Gesture
**Players:** pairs, with the whole class alternating roles
**Setup:** open space
**Goal:** trains allowing the mind to work by itself, accepting the idea that presents itself rather than scouring for originality, which makes working together effortless
**Rules:** 1. Stage one (deliberately "stupid"): "Ann, raise your arm. Brian, look at her for one second. Brian, return her 'to neutral'. Ann, shower him with gratitude." Then Brian makes an abstract gesture, perhaps putting his hand on the top of his head, and Ann looks at him for a moment, returns him to neutral, and is thanked profusely. All students try the procedure, alternating between making an abstract gesture and having the partner cancel it. 2. Stage two: play as before, or justify the gesture. If a finger is being pointed you could pretend to manicure it, if someone covers their ears you could mime turning down the stereo. Don't force ideas, just be mildly interested as to whether an idea presents itself or not. 3. If no idea comes, return your partner to neutral, because you get thanked anyway. 4. Thank each other more, we need enthusiasm. Sample exchanges: Ann extends a hand and Brian kisses it. Brian raises his arm and Ann strap-hangs on a train. Ann extends her arms, Brian pretends to photograph her, and she becomes a model and thanks him.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Take the spectators' attitude: they are under no pressure to be right, so their imaginations work automatically, and an extended palm reads as catching a ball, testing for rain, or wanting a palm read. If hesitation comes from wanting to be original ("I had an impulse to lift a small bird from her finger but remembered someone had done that earlier"), insist: accept the idea that presents itself, we've only got two arms and two legs, and besides, it's not the same bird. Treat ideas as gifts showered on you, like the mise-en-scene that replaces itself page after page as you read a novel, so creativity becomes attending rather than thinking. Master this and cooperation is automatic: you point, and your servant shoots the bird you had no idea you were pointing at.

## 3. He Said/She Said
**Players:** pairs (introduced by the coach with two volunteers while the group watches)
**Setup:** open space
**Goal:** trains controlling your partner through narration, and being controlled, without erecting walls against intrusion (includes the follow-up section "Defences")
**Rules:** 1. Tell your partner what to do, and then add a line of dialogue. 2. Model exchange: "I'm home!" "He said, throwing his satchel on to the table. 'How was school?'" "She said, picking it up and looking inside it. 'Mum! That's mine!'" "He said, trying to pull it away from me but failing miserably. 'I just want to see what you have for your homework.'" "She said, emptying it out on to the table. 'I finished my homework at school, Mum.'" "He said, giving me a phoney smile and edging towards the door. 'I talked to your teacher today.'" "She said, grabbing me by the neck. 'Whatever for?'" "He said, turning pale and refusing to look at me. 'You haven't been to school for a month. You told your teacher we had moved to another city!'" 3. Introduce it when students are in a good mood, explaining that it's easy for small children but difficult for adults, which arouses curiosity and lessens self-punishment. Lead volunteers through it, feeding the dialogue: 'Say "Hallo! Max!"' Linda says it. Tell Max: 'Just say "She said ..." then "Embracing me"', assembling "She said, embracing me." Linda embraces him saying "Nice to see you," and you correct: "Not yet. First he has a line of dialogue, then you tell him what to do." 4. Defences: players soon find subtle ways to minimize interaction, controlling from a distance ("He said, going to the door," "She said, climbing on a chair and flapping her arms"). Point this out, because it's more fun to work closely together: "You swine!" "She said, throwing me to the floor and kneeling on my chest. 'Augh! You're hurting me!'" "He said, loving it. 'It's your turn to wash the dishes!'" "She said, dragging me to the sink ..." 5. Watch for the common defences of describing what the partner is already doing ("He said, breathlessly"), giving yourself the stage directions ("He said, cowering away from me as I kicked the door shut and produced my amazing weapon"), or expanding dialogue into whole paragraphs so the partner can't control you. Prevent paragraphing with the Three Word Sentence Game (see p. 155), and correct minimal control ("She said, sipping her tea") by shouting additions: "She said, sipping her tea, and realizing that it tasted strange," or "He said, looking bored and starting to undress."
**Variations:** Gibberish version: experts use gibberish dialogue ("Enyam regor?" "He said, sitting on the sofa, and taking my hand. 'Ocil lej?'") while continuing to give the instructions in English, and bizarrely find it less confusing. Shadows version: two players supply the dialogue while two other "shadows" stay very close and supply the stage directions, which encourages bolder scenes because players feel less alone. Audience volunteers version: the players give stage directions to a volunteer as well as to each other. Pimping version, preferred before raucous audiences: "He said, bursting into song," "He said, reading The Fall of the Roman Empire ... aloud," which gets laughs but turns the game into pure filler where no story emerges. Adjective version: supply adjectives instead of stage directions, e.g. "Sorry I'm late (SUSPICION)." "You're late rather a lot these days, what's going on? (PRIDE)." "I know where you've been. I followed you (CONTEMPT)."
**Coaching:** The game sounds coercive, but your partner can't really control you against your will ("There are no more patients, Doctor." "She said, unbuttoning her blouse. 'Nurse Kimble, stop it at once!'" "He said, buttoning it up for me ..."). It was suggested by actors at Tournus (Denmark), who named it "He Said, Removing His Trousers," and Johnstone's three-year-old learned it instantly because toddlers are used to being handled and eager for revenge. When confusion erupts, spell the rules out again and conclude: "So the confusion lies in us, not in the instructions. Don't get disheartened, it takes about forty minutes to master this game." Note that the comical is often identical to the horrific, as in the brain-stealing surgeon sequence played with three-word sentences.

## 4. Substitution Impro
**Players:** two starting players plus any number of substituting players (class or audience)
**Setup:** a stage with whatever the chosen scene needs
**Goal:** trains confident, unplanned playing, since the situation continues even though the actors keep changing, the exact opposite of the Freeze Game
**Rules:** 1. Set up a master-servant scene or a boy-girl scene, and get the class to agree that the players will keep being substituted. 2. At first few substitutions occur, but the agreement lets you shout "replace them" and even push students physically into the scene. 3. Match the posture you inherit: if someone is on their knees, kneel as you replace them (a tap on the shoulder tells them to leave), if they are mid-sentence you should complete it, if their arms are outspread your arms should be outspread. 4. Sustain established voices through the scene: if one character speaks in a foreign accent and another in upper-class English, keep those dialects. 5. Expect visual overload at high frequency: players substituted every few seconds, a couple about to kiss changed so often the action goes into extreme slow motion, a shot character recast while falling, even the corpse morphing while the murderer tries to cover up the crime.
**Variations:** Battle of the Sexes: a dispute between a man and a woman, men substitute the man and women the woman, with the women cheering for the woman and the men for the man, which can generate so much enthusiasm that spectators run onstage (Johnstone recalls a "husband" saying cuttingly "Oh, it's you!", a woman rushing from the audience to replace the "wife" with "Did you think it was your mistress?", and a male spectator leaping up with "Married to you, who can afford a mistress?"). Don't let men substitute for women or vice versa outside this variant, it's a gag that stops the game being taken seriously. Quieter versions exist: start with someone sitting alone in the park who is approached by a stranger, perhaps they leave together and the scene continues in a cafe or someone's house. Also known as tag-team impro, satisfying the spectator's urge to leap onstage and say or do the thing the players failed to do.
**Coaching:** An excellent performance game, really more suitable for Free-Impro because it's less exhausting with more players, and it can occur spontaneously as a way of ending a scene. Johnstone introduced it because proud beginners wouldn't signal for help, and forced substitution made them wonderfully confident: why plan or worry about doing well when you know you're about to be replaced? Once substitutions reach a certain frequency everyone, including absolute beginners, is eager to leap in, a bit like starting a cold motor.

## 5. Moving Bodies
**Players:** pairs, one "puppet" and one "puppeteer" per pair
**Setup:** two chairs a few feet apart for the puppets, and real furniture: the scenes are not suitable for bare stages, and sofas offer more possibilities than chairs, so the Judges should say "Some furniture please!"
**Goal:** trains shared control of a scene, isolation of body parts, and justifying imposed movement
**Rules:** 1. The characters invent their own dialogue, but their bodies are operated like puppets by other people (when Roddy Maude Roxby saw the original at Jerome Robbins' studio the puppeteers dictated the words too, but Johnstone insisted the control be shared). 2. Sit two puppets on chairs and start them as strangers in some public place, a park or a cruise ship. Warn the operators not to yank the puppets about or put them in painful positions. 3. Movements should be justified: if your puppeteer points your finger, say something like "What's that over there?" or "Look at this ladybird on my finger!" Conversely, if you say "Look at that!", you can hope your puppeteer points your finger for you. 4. Only the part of the body being moved should move, don't "help" your puppeteer, and shout reminders like "Just the arm," "Not the neck!" or "Only move the part that's being moved!", because puppets must isolate parts that habitually function as a unit. 5. A puppeteer who wants the puppet to tap its foot must keep on tapping it, the puppet won't take over the movement. 6. Errors should be justified: if your head has been left facing down say "Footprints!" or "I'm too ashamed to look you in the eye!", if you are toppling say "Quick! My heart medication!" or "I think we need a mural on that ceiling," and if your head faces somewhere inappropriate drop hints: "A lovely view." "Yes, I only wish I could see it."
**Variations:** With audience volunteers as puppeteers: creates enormous benevolence, ask them to be gentle, express hope that some sort of story emerges, remind them that elbows don't bend backward, fingers stay away from orifices, and that they should step back sometimes rather than move the puppets non-stop. If a puppet is reduced to a talking head it should say things like "I'll just stand up and walk over to the stereo," or "Shall we dance?"
**Coaching:** Puppeteers "forget" parts of the puppet, leaving an arm sticking out or walking it leg by leg with the head twisted sideways. Allow this in performance unless the puppet seems in pain, but don't let puppeteers be stupid deliberately, the accidents are funny enough. Beginner puppeteers forget that legs must be beneath us when we stand, and one pleasure is watching a small puppeteer handle a large puppet threatening to overbalance. The game is so entertaining that some players want it at the end of every performance, discourage this or the shows become predictable and the laughs too easy.

## 6. Explore the Possibilities
**Players:** a Moving Bodies troupe, potentially with audience helpers
**Setup:** as Moving Bodies, plus room to move through the auditorium
**Goal:** generating new versions of Moving Bodies
**Rules:** 1. Start from real events: when a Moving Bodies scene turned into a flying lesson, six people ran out of the audience to help "fly" the student around the auditorium. 2. Invent further scenarios, for example a puppet tormented by questions of free will, or a puppet who is aware of the puppeteers and is diagnosed as insane.
**Variants to avoid:** Some cowardly impro groups cast audience volunteers as the puppets. This is not what the audience wants to see, and few volunteers have the skill to isolate the part of the body being moved.
**Coaching:** None given

## 7. The Arms
**Players:** pairs labelled A and B (A supplies the voice and body, B supplies the arms), later combining into larger configurations
**Setup:** open space for the party stage, chairs or a table for the teacher stage, audience seating facing the players for Two Sets of Arms
**Goal:** trains instant cooperation and shared control, and works as a sensitivity game allowing people to invade each other's space without feeling threatened (from Laurel and Hardy, now one of the most popular impro games in the world)
**Rules:** 1. Party Version: "Find a partner. Call yourselves A and B. Bs stand behind As with your arms thrust under their arms. The As should put their hands behind the Bs' backs. Each pair is to become one person with A supplying the voice and B doing the gestures." 2. Coach the pair to break taboos in stages: can the Bs touch the As' hair while the As move their heads a little as if doing the touching themselves, could the Bs touch the As' faces, could they adjust the As' clothing (this evokes the most laughter, sweaters tugged about, necklines adjusted). 3. Set the scene: "Be at a party. Meet old friends! Find out where the drinks are! Shuffle about and introduce yourselves to strangers! Mingle!" Gaze out of the window so nobody feels scrutinized, and after twenty seconds or so make everyone change partners, since each change makes the students more alert. 4. Arms Teacher stage: select one pair of arms to address the group: "Be a teacher. But establish that the class is about to end so that the moment you feel the need to be 'clever' or 'original' you can just say, 'Class dismissed,' or 'For homework tonight I want you to ...' and we'll fade the lights." Encourage the arms to point at audience members while the body says things like "Tutti! Are you cheating?" or "Suzanne! Put your hands on the desk where I can see them!" or "Etienne? Did you throw that paper aeroplane?" 5. Next tell the pair to imagine a table in front of them, and B mimes picking up items that A describes: "The sex life of snails is a closed book to many people, but I have here a male snail, called Nat." The left hand displays Nat. "And I have here a female snail, called Betsy." The right hand mimes lifting Betsy. "Calm down, Nat! Stop it, Betsy! Just a moment, I'd better tie on the little blindfolds." And so on. 6. Two Sets of Arms stage: take two pairs of arms and ask them to face the audience, explaining that if they want to approach each other they'll have to move crab-wise because the illusion works best seen from the front. Ask for an interaction between strangers, a problem, a possible tilt, and a solution, for instance two strangers at a late-night bus stop discovering the last bus has gone, until one realizes the other was the school bully and can be bullied into sharing a taxi. 7. Loading the Pockets is a corrupt version: the pockets are crammed with objects which the arms produce one by one while the body comments, reducing the game to pure filler with no narrative.
**Variations:** In the tobacco days, have the arms light a cigarette for the body to smoke, excellent for gigs where the audience spoke no English. Shaving the face with foam and an empty razor might be fun.
**Coaching:** If players ignore their partners and start planning, encourage them to respond to what's actually happening. Common sense says the game would be easier if either A or B were in charge, but the control should be shared: if A says "I have here a stick of gelignite!" the arms should pick up a stick of gelignite, and if B's hand covers A's mouth, A should yawn. Some of the best moments in improvisation occur when no one is sure who's leading and who's following. Avoid Loading the Pockets, and especially the unpleasant spectacle of improvisers cramming their pockets before a scene: one object would be enough, but at the moment the improviser might move into the future (inspired by a rubber knife to commit suicide, or by a peanut to expound the problems of living with an elephant) the arms sidetrack by presenting a new object.

## 8. The Dwarf
**Players:** two performers, A above and B providing arms below
**Setup:** boots on A's hands, cloth covering A's chest so we just see the boots projecting underneath (build a special costume), a table or sofa for the dwarf to stand behind
**Goal:** two-person illusion performance and physical comedy
**Rules:** 1. B's arms protrude under A's arms, A's hands are thrust into boots, and the cloth hides the join, producing the effect of a misshapen creature about three feet high standing just behind a table or sofa. 2. Let it "walk" about on the table or sofa-back, clamber up after "falling" over the edge, dance about, do the splits, lie on its side resting on one elbow, flap its arms to rise into the air, sit with a bump, and twist its foot three hundred and sixty degrees (or let someone else twist it).
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Originally a party trick from Johnstone's youth, used in improvisations and plays. Dwarves work well when they're cheeky, extrovert characters.

## 9. The Giant
**Players:** two performers, A the upper body and B the legs
**Setup:** a bench, cloth bags for A's legs, a costume covering both bodies
**Goal:** two-person illusion performance
**Rules:** 1. B lies on the bench with bare legs dangling over the end, feet towards the audience. A faces front and stands astride B, A's legs hidden in cloth bags, the costume covering both bodies to give the effect of an eight-foot-high person who happens to be sitting down. 2. Sometimes the giant should lean one way while its legs point the other way, so it seems inherently unstable, and you can add instability by having the body lean back and wave its arms for balance while the feet lift into the air. 3. Give it business: it can force people to do exercises, touching its own toes by letting its feet swing up to meet its hands, it can bully its servants, it can be a terrible coward who gloats over the misfortunes of others.
**Variations:** Johnstone used two giants as the High Priests when he directed the Wakefield Cycle.
**Coaching:** Make sure the giant is always loud and exuberant.

## 10. Adjective
**Players:** pairs or groups
**Setup:** none
**Goal:** explores how a single applied quality colours a whole scene, provided it is justified
**Rules:** 1. Apply an adjective to a scene, for example the slow-family, the fit-family, the evil-family, the suspicious-family, the religious-family, the silly-family, the right-wing-family. 2. Or set up a pecking order of bigots, or start a suspicious master-servant scene.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Stanislavsky said "Never play adjectives," but it's okay if you justify them.

## 11. Wide Eyes
**Players:** individuals in scenes, then the whole group, then pairs
**Setup:** space for scenes, and a party setup for the en masse version
**Goal:** opens and relaxes the players, making them more open, vulnerable and uninhibited, diminishing self-consciousness and arousing protective feelings in the audience
**Rules:** 1. Improvise a scene with your eyes as wide open as possible, keeping the lids raised even when you glance down. 2. Wide Eyes en Masse: set up a thirty-second "party" at which everyone has tight eyes, then discuss the sensations. Then hold a party in which all the guests have wide-open "innocent" eyes, noticing how in both cases self-consciousness diminishes because everyone concentrates on what they're doing rather than on your opinion of them. Then hold a party mixing narrow-eyed people with wide-eyed people. 3. Wide Eyes, Friends Meet: before you mention eyes, play a twenty-second scene in which two great friends meet after a long separation, then get them to repeat it with eyes wide open, and the difference may be astounding, with more enthusiasm, larger gestures, and less fear of the space. Tell them that if they glance "out front" occasionally they'll notice most of the onlookers also have wide eyes, it's infectious.
**Variations:** the mixed narrow-eyed and wide-eyed party
**Coaching:** We all had wide eyes in early childhood, and when we wanted something they became so enormous it was difficult to refuse us, but since then many of us close our lids defensively. Ask students to notice Harpo's eyes, or Goldie Hawn's, or Benny Hill's, or Nina Hagen's, and explain that tight eyes may be okay for baddies but don't encourage us to feel positive and outgoing. Warning: some professional actors open the eyes unnaturally wide, a "blob on a boiled egg" effect which is occasionally useful but is the equivalent of shouting all the time. The eye that is open, trusting and friendly has the pupil fully exposed with no white above or below it. Learn to open your eyes by relaxing them rather than pitting one set of muscles against another: try lying down, letting go, while trying to sense the exact weight of your eyeballs, which can be emotional.

## 12. Wide Mouth
**Players:** individual students, then in scenes
**Setup:** none
**Goal:** explores how mouth shape changes status, boldness, and fear of the audience
**Rules:** 1. Have students project their voices very loudly while making their mouths narrower than usual, which releases laughter and makes them extremely bossy. 2. Then go for the rebound effect: widen the mouth slightly, perhaps parting the lips slightly, and look at the objects and people around you. You'll probably feel bolder, some people feel as if they could "eat the world," and fear of the audience lessens just as in the Wide Eye procedure. This is not grinning or smiling but a widening of about a quarter of an inch, and it's the intention to widen that creates the effect. 3. Play status through teeth: high-status people expose the bottom teeth (Jimmy Carter can do this even looking friendly and good natured) whereas low-status people expose the top teeth. Try biting the bottom lip, raising the upper lip and giving a stupid giggle, noticing how the back of the neck wants to shorten. 4. Expose both sets of teeth and breathe audibly: you may want to smash things (don't), the space to the sides becomes more important, elbows tend to move sideways and the body tends to rotate left and right a little. Use the effect for thugs or executioners, and note that an executioner's black hood hiding everything but the mouth and chin intensifies it.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** The man who auditioned dancers for the Crazy Horse nightclub in Paris said that when in doubt he opted for the ones with "big mouths," and many models and dynamic people seem to have extra teeth. Everyday mouths contracted so far that one wonders what they are refusing. The widened state releases positive behaviour, a sort of Mel Gibson in Lethal Weapon dogginess.

## 13. Tempo
**Players:** pairs, groups, or threesomes
**Setup:** open space
**Goal:** separates dramatic speed from physical speed and trains players to hold independent tempos
**Rules:** 1. Establish that dramatic speed is related to the pace at which ideas are changing: a marathon runner might be dramatically slow, while a sentry standing at attention approached by a dog that cocks its leg might be dramatically fast, even if you only know it by the sweat breaking out on his forehead. 2. Ask a "slow" player to have a new thought every thirty seconds and a "fast" player a new thought every five seconds, as in the airport scene where the slow player drifts ("I like flying ...", "I like to get a window seat.", "If you're going to be cooped up ...", "You might as well have a good view.", "Of course, if it's cloudy ...") while the fast player panics ("Where's my ticket? I left it in my ... No! It's up in the bathroom. Did I pack my razor?", "Perhaps it's in the suitcase! But which suitcase?", "Did I pack the alarm-clock?", "No time to shave! Do I look respectable?", "Passport! Where is it?", "That's the old one. Ah! It's in my pocket!", "Shampoo! I'll get some from the bathroom.", "Did you phone the airline?", "Oh, God, I'll do it myself. The phone book!"). 3. Ask groups to change ideas at the same pace, perhaps a slow picnic that gradually accelerates into a fast picnic, where the changes of pace need to be justified.
**Variations:** Pairs in which one player changes from slow to fast and the other from fast to slow, asking them to control the rate of change so they "pass" each other midway. Threesomes in which A maintains a neutral tempo, B moves from fast to slow, and C moves from slow to fast, leaving the stage just before exploding.
**Coaching:** Beginners get security by joining each other, so if one is fast they all tend to be fast, and simply asking for different speeds gives poor results until thoughts are rationed by the clock. It's always a pleasure seeing actors who do not take the same tempo.

## 14. Sound Scape
**Players:** one player offstage making sounds, plus the onstage players
**Setup:** a microphone, and a collected stock of noise-makers
**Goal:** trains sound-effect work that supports, extends, and comments on the action
**Rules:** 1. A player offstage makes sound effects for the onstage players. The game dates back to one of the first classes at the Studio, when Johnstone clanked tin-cans while someone moved about wearing a mimed suit of armour, and it led to scenes with improvisers and jazz musicians. Use a microphone. 2. Typical sounds include doors creaking and slamming, taps or showers running, footsteps, voices, cars revving, beds creaking, animals barking, howling and whining, chalk screeching on blackboards, bones or muscles cracking as players stretch, metal vibrating ("doooinggg!"), things being inflated and deflating, fists smashing, yawns, sneezes and tuts, and emotional sounds like panting, gulping, sighing, weeping, and laughing. 3. The game can also involve dubbing the dialogue, although that would be unusual: Jasmine mimes opening a door (creak) and closing it (slam), walks to the shelf (footsteps), mimes removing a huge tin (scrape), mimes opening it (wrenching sounds plus the whining of a huge hungry animal), pours the contents on to the floor (slurping sounds), pats the huge thing making the slurping and panting noises (patting sounds) and says "Did you have fun today, darling?" and a huge voice on the mike says "Yes, I chased the postman!"
**Variations:** Sounds can be accepted physically (every joint can creak, and your digestive system can make strange noises), placed in the environment (the undertakers' sign is creaking in the wind), or used to establish mood, although music is better at that.
**Coaching:** Collect noise-makers.

## 15. You're Interesting
**Players:** pairs, with the coach interrupting and feeding suggestions
**Setup:** open space
**Goal:** a way of marking time on stage while seeming alive and interested, which reverses the beginner's habits of ignoring others and lowering expectations, and diminishes future-funk
**Rules:** 1. Follow three rules: feel fit and awake, be interested in everything that the others say or do, and say nothing interesting, while avoiding being so uninteresting that you become interesting (by talking about nothing but cabbages, for example). 2. If a line creates no effect, interrupt: "She said, 'Good morning,' so be interested! Look surprised! Let it have some effect on you." 3. Restart the scene. They react to "Good morning!" with "So it is!", and when that is ignored, explain that whoever said "Good morning" is obliged by the rules to make the "So it is!" interesting as well, which produces the sheepish "Sorry I'm a bit late!" 4. If that line also creates no effect, suggest "I'm glad you managed to get here," or "Yes, we kept some cake for you," but delete remarks like "Did you have an accident?" as too interesting. 5. Develop the story by throwing in the occasional interesting idea: "Say, 'I've been promoted,'" or "Say, 'I've joined the police force.'" 6. Continue applying the rules to whatever emerges, as in the extended sample dialogue: "A policeman! That must be so interesting." "[Brightly] Really? Well, I suppose it must look glamorous from the outside, but it's all run of the mill. Parking tickets ... interrogations ..." "Interrogations? Goodness!" "You're interested? Well, that's encouraging. But it's mostly just chatting to citizens who don't quite know their place." "Oh! Not people like me, then? Hah! Hah!" "No, no, just social misfits. I look on it as further education." "Further education? Really! The teacher is always right, eh?" "What did you say?" "[Alarmed] Er ... what did you think I said?" "[Suave] I thought you said something very interesting. Drink up!" "Drink up? Er, are we going anywhere?" "We're having a party at Headquarters." "[Suppressed panic] But I couldn't er ..." "Of course you can. It's just round the corner." "Um ... er ... [Gulp]" Reject the closing line, "Drinks are on us. And you'll get a 'Help Your Local Police, Beat Yourself Up' T-shirt," as too interesting.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** When beginners try to think of clever things to say they pay scant attention to anyone else and look feeble and unsure to lower our expectations, and being interested reverses these tactics. Players who say dull things are likely to look bored, so remind them to be healthy and alert and to respond with interest to whatever is said. Once students obey the instructions they look wonderfully alive and fearless, and their interest makes us interested. Struggling to say dull things while being interested is more entertaining than the usual "I'm the wittiest" interaction.

## 16. Boring the Audience
**Players:** players of an already admired acting scene, plus the coach
**Setup:** any staged scene that has been rehearsed or performed
**Goal:** removes the pressure to "do your best" and allows something other than the social personality to operate
**Rules:** 1. Take the players of an admired scene aside and whisper something to them. 2. Ask them to play the scene again. 3. The secret instruction is that they should bore the audience without being bored themselves. 4. The second presentation will almost certainly seem noticeably improved, and sometimes the actors seem wonderfully talented, revealing a truth and absorption never seen in them before. 5. Enjoy the onlookers' wrong guesses about what you whispered ("To be interesting? To be expressive? To be more relaxed? To pay attention to each other? To be truthful? To have a subtext? To have a purpose?") and the players' amazement, sometimes total disbelief, that their attempt to bore us made them more interesting.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Most students were dreary when they did their best but became fascinating when content to be just average, hence Johnstone's old shouted instruction "Be more boring!" at the Studio. William Wyler stopped Olivier from emoting by reading the newspaper while making him do the same shot as many as fifty times, and Olivier was grateful, but telling him to "bore the camera" might have wasted less film.

## 17. Wallpaper Drama
**Players:** pairs
**Setup:** open space
**Goal:** a training game spun off from work on transitions, useful for improvised soap operas, that sustains interest while marking time on stage
**Rules:** 1. Morph between positive and negative, always going through neutral, and always giving all three states the same value. 2. Work through the cycle using a concrete situation, for example the birthday gift scene. Positive: "Hey, great to see you." "Isn't it your birthday?" "You remembered!" "I bought this for you." "Oh, you shouldn't have!" Moving towards neutral: "You'll like it. It's an egg-whisk." "Ah well, I can always use another one. It's the thought that counts." "Yes, well, I was thinking of you." Moving towards negative: "It's my twenty-first birthday. And what do you bring me? A bloody egg-whisk." "If you don't want it, I'll have it back." "I want it!" "Show some gratitude then!" Moving towards neutral: "Yes, well, I'm sorry." "The thing is, I didn't have any money. Actually, I took the egg-whisk out of the kitchen drawer." "Stole it from your mum?" "I wanted to give you something." Moving towards positive: "We could make some egg-nog." "Great!" And so on. 3. The coach may need to prod the scene occasionally, for example "Tell him you're pregnant," and then, "Tell him it's not his."
**Variations:** More tricks for soap operas (not Johnstone's): enter a circle of light at one side of the stage, and/or touch the proscenium, to explain privately to the audience how you feel, go to the opposite side of the stage to supply narrative and/or explanations, and have an object that you touch when you want the audience to tell you the next line.
**Coaching:** The name comes from doing it well: you can burble on for ever and yet sustain some sort of interest. Left to themselves beginners move between negative and neutral and avoid being positive, so don't let this happen.

## 18. Straight Men
**Players:** pairs, a comic and a straight man
**Setup:** optionally a Velcroed letter "S" badge supplied by the scenographers
**Goal:** builds the neglected skill of playing the serious foil, and teaches strong contrast between performers
**Rules:** 1. Establish the classic pairing: the straight man is a long-suffering "parental" figure and the comic is a pesky "child" who annoys them, as with Bert and Ernie on Sesame Street, or ventriloquists and their dummies, and note that in public improvisation there are very few straight men. 2. Have the scenographers stick a Velcroed "S" (for Straight man) on any improviser who is compulsively gagging, and tell the audience that this player is to be serious and will be dragged away by the gag-police if he or she makes any jokes. 3. Insist on strong contrast between the straight man and the comic, citing Jerry Lewis and Dean Martin, or Margaret Dupont and Groucho Marx. 4. Illustrate with Bernie Winters' account of Mike Winters commissioning a tailor to work through the night on a crazy Teddy boy outfit with drainpipe trousers, heavily padded shoulders and a velvet collar, designed to make Bernie look like a sack of potatoes moving across the stage: he stumbled on with the suit and big hat, ran up and down, and the audience howled before either had opened his mouth, his nervousness triggering the "ee" giggle which they roared at, and after that they could not go wrong, because the bulky suit made the perfect contrast to Mike's immaculate suit.
**Variations:** None given
**Coaching:** Being a straight man is a skill worth acquiring, and should be taught at the same time as "being a passenger".
