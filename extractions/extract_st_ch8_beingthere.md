# Chapter 8: Being There. Extracted Exercises and Games

## Three-word Sentences

**Players:** 2+ scene partners

**Setup:** Ordinary two-person scene, except every sentence must be exactly three words.

**Goal:** Stop beginners leaving their mouths on 'automatic' while their minds gallop into the future. Improve rapport, prevent gags, calm panicky improvisers.

**Rules:**
1. Every sentence must be exactly three words, complete, no trailing off.
2. Adjust phrasing to fit: instead of 'Come in!' say 'Come in please,' or 'That you, honey?'
3. Keep playing the scene under this constraint.

**Variations:** None given.

**Coaching:** The imposed slowness can be read as emotional involvement, use it. Gags become almost impossible.

## One-word Sentences

**Players:** 2

**Setup:** Two players have greeted each other and get stuck. Trainer supplies activities: 'Open a cupboard!', 'Take something out of your pocket and show it to him.'

**Goal:** Force non-verbal physical solutions instead of verbal ones.

**Rules:**
1. Each spoken line is restricted to one word.
2. Meaning must be carried by mimed objects and actions.
3. His example: Eric opens the cupboard, takes a tray, sets it on the table. 'Brandy?' 'Please!' He pours two brandies. 'Cheers!' Stuck again, Renee mimes taking something from her pocket and shows Eric. He takes it gingerly, sights along the barrel. 'Fired?' he asks, sniffing it. 'Recently,' says Renee. He places it on the table. 'Husband?' She wants to say 'I shot him between the eyes' but manages 'Dead,' taking the gun back.

**Variations:** Coached Word Length (below).

**Coaching:** Coach until players cotton on, most improvisers default to verbal solutions. The resistance to speech makes them seem emotional.

## Coached Word Length

**Players:** 2+, trainer shouting numbers

**Setup:** Ordinary scene. Trainer shouts a number of words instead of an emotion.

**Goal:** Stretch sentences on cue. His verdict: audience laughs and applauds, but nothing happens, so not recommended.

**Rules:**
1. Trainer shouts e.g. 'FOUR!' Player: 'Can I help you?'
2. Trainer shouts 'TEN!' Other player: 'Perhaps you can assist me with a small problem . . . sir.'

**Variations:** None.

**Coaching:** Johnstone advises against it.

## The Hat-Game

**Players:** 2 per round, Judges, optional Commentator

**Setup:** Both wear easily removable hats. Trilby or fedora best, keep a dent in the top. Worn fairly level, not jammed over ears, not shading eyes. No large floppy hats, no peaked caps (fingers near eyes). Play a scene.

**Goal:** Secretly seize your partner's hat. Win if you take it cleanly, lose if your attempt fails or your own hat gets taken.

**Rules:**
1. Play a scene with the secret intention of taking the partner's hat.
2. Hats are to be taken, not batted into the air. Knocking a hat off doesn't count. Unless you end the grab with the hat in your hand, you lose.
3. Once a grab is attempted the round is over, Judges decide what counts as a grab. Aggressor loses unless the hat is taken cleanly, so you can win by luring a failed attempt.
4. Never mention the hat: no hat-shop encounters, no 'I had a hat like that once'.
5. Beyond arm's length: coach calls 'Risk the hat!' If the scene needs bare heads (barber mimes sheeting you), remove the hat, fan yourself with it, scratch your head, and don't replace it until your partner moves away. Unnecessary removal: Judges say 'Keep the hat in play!'
6. Never manoeuvre where the hat can be taken at will. 50 per cent chance is exciting, 100 per cent boring.
7. Take it when you can: enjoying the scene while ignoring an available hat is bridging and wastes the game.
8. Hold your ground: better to lose the hat than retreat. If you move, move forward, walk into your opponent, tempt an ill-advised grab.
9. Split consciousness: part of the mind plays the scene, another part watches. Planners die: 'There's something on your shoe!' or the migraine excuse keeps experienced players alert. Grandma asked 'What year were you born?' retreats inward to subtract 163, and her hat is wafted gently away.
10. Scoring: usual challenge is best out of three, one-on-one, each hat three points. One-off games: Judges point at the winner, five points.

**Teaching stages:**
1. Student takes a hat slowly from a non-resisting head. From the side or above, not the front ('you don't want your little finger stuck up a nostril'). Speed up once they've got the distance.
2. One tries to take, the wearer protects by grabbing first or dodging.
3. Both wear hats, each takes the other's the instant opportunity appears. Add dialogue, move about: play scenes.
4. Add roles: nuns, brain surgeons, cans of baked beans, mice, politicians at an apathy conference.
5. A winning taker continues round after round, optionally with a Commentator narrating ('That's Karen's fourth consecutive hat . . .').

**Variations:** Giving the Hat: bare-headed players each hold a hat and try to place it on the partner. Original is funnier because the loser's status drops visibly. Bum Tag: touch the other's behind first to win; sitting down or backing to a wall earns 'Keep the buttocks in play!' Good mass warm-up. Blindfold Hat-Games: colleagues steer the blindfolded players, audiences love it, potentially dangerous, wear goggles, never beginners; street version barefoot among mouse-traps (The Three Canadians).

**Improvements to reject:** Whistle rules deciding when the hat may be taken achieve nothing. The 'West Coast' ban on grabbing your own hat arose from one brief Vancouver demo and spread worldwide, killing the game: ducking, weaving and grabbing your own first is the humour. Requiring the grab to be justified within the scene trains planning and made one group quit as boring.

**Coaching:** 'Look after your partner! Be gentle! Think quick and light, not heavy and strong.' Anxiety makes students inept. Detach ego: 'What's it matter? It's not even your hat! Take risks! Win or lose, either way we'll be entertained!' Losers must not be indifferent: look astonished, confused, or laugh at yourself, we love to see one person 'lowered' by another. If onlookers don't laugh when hats are grabbed, something is wrong. Gaskill found the game made written scenes so witty he thought he'd 'found the secret of clowning'. A Zen monk was amazed to hear it called mindfulness training.

## Gaskill's Samurai Game

**Players:** 2

**Setup:** Two players face each other, motionless. Invented around 1960 by William Gaskill to discourage planning, inspired by the Seven Samurai duel.

**Goal:** Touch the opponent before being touched, with zero visible preparation. Train body-decision over planning.

**Rules:**
1. Neither moves a muscle until one, giving no signal, reaches out and touches the other, and wins.
2. If the touched player leaps back so contact isn't made, the attacker loses.
3. Any defensive or aggressive movement that fails restarts the game.
4. Hat version: both wear hats a couple of feet apart, either wins by taking the other's hat. No dodging, no feints.
5. If you feel yourself tensing, say 'Pax,' step back, begin the round again.

**Variations:** The hat-taking version above.

**Coaching:** The torso clamps up before the arm moves, and that tension alerts the partner (like the boxer who watched for his opponent's 'decision' and punched first). Interrupt interior dialogue ('Shall I take it now? Not yet! How about now?') by shouting 'Step back! Relax! Calm down!' or suggest repeating 'I want nothing!'. Beginners get hypnotized, eyes flashing 'HAT! HAT! HAT!'. Explain the timing: a spontaneous grab takes a third of a second, faster than the wearer's eye registering, processing and activating muscles. Played right you feel calm, because the body decided.

## Grandmother's Footsteps

**Players:** Class plus one 'grandmother'

**Setup:** Alan faces the wall, the class stands at a starting line. Childhood game, also called Red Light Green Light or Statues.

**Goal:** Advance without being caught moving. Warm-up for Making Faces.

**Rules:**
1. Class creeps towards Alan.
2. He looks round suddenly: anyone he sees moving returns to the starting line.
3. Whoever gets close enough to touch him, or the wall, ends the game.

**Variations:** None given.

**Coaching:** Use as warm-up before Making Faces.

## Making Faces, Party Version

**Players:** Whole group

**Setup:** Group spreads out pretending to be at a party: recognize old friends, exchange gossip, circulate, find refreshments.

**Goal:** Make faces at others unseen while catching others making faces.

**Rules:**
1. Try to make faces at each other without being caught.
2. Point your fingers and say 'bang' to shoot anyone you catch making a face at you.
3. If shot, you leave the game.

**Variations:** Johnstone always wanted to play it on a lawn with water-pistols.

**Coaching:** Keep the party frame alive.

## Making Faces in Threes (Master and Two Servants)

**Players:** 3 seated, plus coach

**Setup:** Three plain chairs facing the audience, edges touching (overlapping space helps the comedy). Middle player is the master, outer two the servants. Invented because master-servant pairs failed: masters refused to look away, believing that won.

**Goal:** Servants make secret faces at the master, who tries to catch and fire them. Trains 'seeing' instead of 'planning'.

**Rules:**
1. Master picks anything to be boss of: 'A hospital, a shop, an atomic-power station, a bakery, a school for detectives.' His example: 'All right, I'm in charge of a zoo!' Interact with both zoo-keepers, don't send them away.
2. Master drags ideas from the servants instead of searching inward: start sentences with 'What about . . .' plus an animal, 'What about the wolves? What about the otters?' Or demand they know her mind and hit them with a two-foot airship balloon if they fail (the Keep the Servant on the Hop Game). Sample: 'Torben! What was I about to say?' 'You were about to ask about the snakes, madam!' 'Have they been stretched?' 'Extensively, madam.' 'And the elephants?' 'We've emptied them, madam!' 'Those boys climbing the fence?' 'Eaten, madam.'
3. Master disciplines: make slouching Torben sit up, pull servants about, beat with the balloon. Servants look respectful and obedient. Pamela's smile: 'It's the sheer pleasure of being near you, madam!'
4. Once masters drag ideas and discipline freely: 'Fire any servants you catch making faces at you!' Or pass secret notes to the servants, so the master only cottons on when the audience howls.
5. Caught servants protest innocence but leave if the master insists. Half a dozen volunteers rush to replace a fired servant.
6. Advanced: servants wave to spectators, letting them know what a fool their master is, while the master doesn't know the audience exists.

**Servant rules:** Face only when unobserved, freeze mid-face if caught out. Respect the master's space: resist the urge to lean towards the neck, no distortions you can't reverse instantly, keep hands away from the boss's head. Increase risk but fight desperately to survive, don't get caught on purpose and don't compete for the ugliest face. Elegant play: surviving on a hair's breadth for many minutes.

**Master rules:** Suspicion dawns gradually, find it almost incredible a servant would be so disrespectful. Turn your head mid-phrase rather than finishing sentences before looking. Look directly at one or the other, peripheral-vision watching means servants can't transgress. Share the scene with both servants.

**Variations:** See Fives, Pecking Orders, and Pairs below.

**Coaching:** 'Look at the other servant! Share the scene!' 'Does Klaus seem odd to you? His position. Why does his mouth look frozen?' Klaus, caught mid-face: 'I'm trying to look intelligent, madam.' 'Well, it's not working!' Even if you miss the face, enforce respectful behaviour. Most actors try to impress the audience with face-making skill and get caught at once. Stop before the laughter exhausts itself, let everyone try master.

## Making Faces in Fives

**Players:** 5 per row, arranged U-S-M-S-U

**Setup:** Add a chair to each end of the threes row. Master in the middle, servants either side, under-servants at the ends.

**Goal:** Speed up training and involve more people, adding a second layer of face-making.

**Rules:**
1. Servants make faces at the master as before.
2. Under-servants make faces at the servants.
3. Master fires servants, servants fire under-servants.
4. Masters may report under-servants' misbehaviour to the servants, but seldom fire them directly.
5. When a servant is fired, the under-servant moves up one place.
6. Replacements always start at the U positions.

**Variations:** Pecking Orders (below).

**Coaching:** Same as threes.

## Pecking Orders

**Players:** 3 in a row, U-S-M

**Setup:** Remove a servant and an under-servant from one side of the fives layout, leaving a one-two-three pecking order.

**Goal:** Run a status ladder while everyone makes faces secretly at their superiors.

**Rules:**
1. Play a scenario: rob a bank, or arrive at the beach for a picnic.
2. Making Faces continues underneath throughout.

**Variations:** None given.

**Coaching:** Members of 'clown' pecking orders should stay close together so we see them as one organism.

## Making Faces in Master-Servant Pairs

**Players:** 2

**Setup:** Two-player scene, one master, one servant. Problem: masters won't look away, not wanting to be the straight man.

**Goal:** Keep the game alive despite the master's reluctance.

**Rules:**
1. Coach forces the master to look away constantly: 'Look away to see the time! Go to the cupboard. Look in a drawer. Drop something and bend to pick it up. Throw a dart and walk past the servant to retrieve it. Turn away and address someone in the audience. Go to the phone . . .'
2. Minimum one look-away every ten seconds, though they'd prefer every ninety.
3. Play to total insanity: if the master kills the servant and looks away, the corpse still makes faces, and if they meet in hell, the game continues.

**Variants:** Avoid the symmetrical challenge where both make faces at each other. That's joining and gives minimum results: the point is one person mocking another.

**Coaching:** Drive the look-aways hard until looking away is habitual.

## The Vampire

**Players:** 2

**Setup:** One player is a vampire forced into ordinary social contact. His example: a vampire desperately needs to rent a basement apartment from an oblivious landlord.

**Goal:** Teach 'having a resistance': full contortion only when unwatched, instant normality when observed.

**Rules:**
1. When the partner glances away: become totally vampire, contort the face, writhe, hiss, show teeth.
2. The moment the partner glances back: return instantly to normal.
3. Start determined not to bite anyone, then let the resolution crumble.
4. Sample scene: landlord turns away, full vampire. Turns back: 'Were you hissing?' 'Was I?' 'I thought I heard something.' 'My . . . my asthma.' You reel away fighting the urge to bite, landlord turns to find you clutching the door-frame: 'Very solidly built, this house.'
5. Late-stage escalation: jump on the landlord, stagger away burning with embarrassment, weep: 'Oh, God, I'm sorry!' 'All right! I'll lie down and you can drive a stake through me! What else is there to do? Go on, see if I care!' Bite while apologizing profusely: 'I'm bleeding! You bit me! You're mad!' 'Yes, and now there are two of us!'

**Variations:** None given.

**Coaching:** Imagine you're a vegetarian for whom biting would be disgusting, or a teacher frantic to keep her job under a suspicious headmaster's interrogation. Not a frequent performance piece, once a year perhaps, mainly a class game for resistance.

## Leave for the Same Reason

**Players:** 3, seated side by side in a straight line, which deliberately magnifies the problem. Training game, not for performance.

**Setup:** All three must leave for the same reason. Invented because his first students would never leave before a scene ended.

**Goal:** Teach cooperation, acceptance of offers, and freedom from hunting 'original ideas'.

**Rules:**
1. Spoken round: one declares a reason, 'It's hot here!', and all three exit because of the heat. Again: 'There are too many ants here!' and all exit to escape ants.
2. Silent round: no dialogue. Each watches the others, copies any small movement, amplifies it together, and the shared reason for leaving emerges from the accumulated behaviour.
3. Take any idea: 'Ideas are an ocean to splash about in. Don't be selective.'
4. Repeat until it seems nothing special.

**His examples:** Failure state: one extends a hand (rain), one scratches (fleas), one applauds (show over), nobody left for the same reason. Successes: nose-wrinkling leads to investigating a delicious smell, blinking to bright light or pollution, sighing to throwing earth in a grave because the funeral's over, a mimed giant fish dragging all three offstage, hair-adjusting becomes hair falling out in shreds, foot-tapping grows into tap-dancing off, coughing means gas masks and poisoned air, feeding a bird becomes hundreds of attacking birds. Gibberish version, or dialogue unrelated to the exit: 'Great game last night!' palm testing for rain, raincoat, umbrella, 'That last goal was spectacular.'

**Variations:** Gibberish speech, or dialogue contradicting the physical exit.

**Coaching:** 'It's no use staring straight ahead, you'll have to see what the others are doing!' 'See what's happening, and do what the others do.' Side-coach ignored offers instantly: 'Maria wrinkled her nose, wrinkle yours. He's moving his neck, move your neck. Now groan. Leave in agony needing medical treatment!' Repetition allowed: 'No one said you have to be original. If you leave for the same reason every time, we'll admire your good nature.'

## Dubbing: One Voice

**Players:** Two or more groups, any size, larger is easier. Each group is one character.

**Setup:** Groups stand along opposite walls, packed close, checking each other constantly. A spin-off from Word-at-a-Time games. At a Svenborg conference each character had 250 people and worked flawlessly.

**Goal:** Improvise whole dialogue in unison, proving that being obvious is enough.

**Rules:**
1. Everyone in a group speaks simultaneously as one character.
2. Say the obvious: original contributions get drowned out.
3. If a line comes out unintelligible, the other group says 'Whaaaat?'

**Teaching sequence:**
1. Kneel to diminish anxiety: 'Can you say what I say, as I say it?' Drawl 'Gooood mooorning' with exaggerated lip movements. Correct echoing to true synchrony.
2. Offer 'It's a . . .' and follow them through 'veeery niiiiceee daaaaay'. You are now following them.
3. Hand the lead around, push the speed, then remove leaders entirely: conversational speed is reachable.
4. Men on one wall, women on the other. Women become one teenage girl, men one teenage boy. Simple checks: 'Toodaay iiisss Friiidaaay!' 'At the Looose Mooose Theeeatre!'
5. Scene: strangers meet, agree to coffee. 'Haallooo therrrre!' 'Hooow arrre yoouuuu?' 'I'mmm fiiiineee!' Tease with unpredictable questions: 'My name is Ssuusshharrlleen!' 'Whaaaaat?' 'Charrrrrlene!' 'Hi, Charrrleeeene!'
6. Family version: ten people as father, ten as mother, ten as the son or daughter who borrowed the Mercedes until midnight and straggles home at four. Prompts: 'It's your fault!', 'You're too soft with that boy!', 'He doesn't take after my side of the family!' Groups swirl and hurl abuse. Fuel with 'Notice that he's drunk!' 'Tell your parents you've decided to be an actor!' 'Walk over and hit him!'

**Variations:** One Voice Parties in Pairs: pairs link arms or shoulder up, each pair one character, at a party, or doctor-patient, parent-child, strangers meeting.

**Coaching:** 'Don't lead! Contribute half a syllable, then wait to see where the group wants the sentence to go. If nothing happens, make a tentative sound.' Loud dominant speakers turn cooperation into obedience, stop them. Afterwards point out that nothing clever was said, yet almost every line drew laughs: people love the obvious, and undefended players look beautiful. The audience is delighted when a sentence collapses into unintelligibility, they don't want flawless experts. Eye contact is a learning-stage instruction only: glance occasionally later, stare only when in trouble.

## The Professor

**Players:** One tightly packed group as the professor, chairman, audience asking questions.

**Setup:** Introduce the group as a professor with a few moments to answer questions on any topic.

**Goal:** Crowd-unison comedy question and answer.

**Rules:**
1. Chairman asks at most one or two questions, then hands over to the audience.
2. Audience fires questions, group answers in one voice.
3. 'Why is the sky blue?' 'Becaaause it's the colour of God's pyyyjaaaamas!'

**Variations:** None given.

**Coaching:** Never let the audience choose the subject: people speaking en masse have a mental age of about three, so 'Ichthyology' and 'quantum physics' wrecked games. 'Keep looking at each other! Don't lead. Don't say anything clever. Be obvious. Remember you're all in the same boat!' Keep it short, end while spectators are hungry for more, weak Judges let it dribble on.

## The Audience Speak

**Players:** Entire audience as one voice, players prompting.

**Setup:** Introduced after the improvisers play a short One Voice Game, so the audience has seen the principle. Born in Gothenburg with 'reserved' Swedes.

**Goal:** The whole theatre improvises in unison.

**Rules:**
1. Ask the audience 'Can you speak in one voice?' Expect 'Yyesssss weeee cannnn!' A 'Noooo weeee caaaan't!' is equally an admission that they can, and they'll laugh thirty seconds at their own cleverness.
2. Build simple scenes where the audience plays one role.

**His examples:** Blind date: players cluster and mime knocking on the fourth wall. 'Aaaanyboooody hoooome?' 'Whoooo's there?' 'It's yooour bliiinnd daaaattee!' 'Goooo awaaaayyyyy!' 'Weee've brought whiiskyy!' 'Cooommmmee innnn!' In Copenhagen the date brought a huge present: 'Do you know what it is?' 'It's aaaa biiiccyyyccclleee!' Other frames: naughty child confessing, 'Whhaaat haaweee yoouu doonnee thiiiis tiimmee?', audience as God interviewing new arrivals in heaven, players issuing the audience a speeding ticket.

**Variations:** Any audience-as-character frame.

**Coaching:** Needs critical mass: worked on the main stage, died in the smaller space, because intellectuals feel obliged to say clever things and a big crowd drowns them out.

## Lip-Sync

**Players:** 2, master speaks, servant mimes. Discovered while teaching ventriloquism and letting the dummy walk about.

**Setup Stage One:** Master picks her own voice and invents a quite different voice for the servant. Nobody leads: if the servant's mouth opens with no voice ready, he yawns or gulps down a passing fly.

**Goal:** One player supplies all dialogue, the other supplies body, training shared control and listening.

**Rules Stage One:**
1. Master calls the servant: 'Angus! Get in here!' Servant replies in, say, Scottish, gesticulating but forgetting to move his lips.
2. Master switches to her own voice: 'Angus! How many times do I have to tell you not to talk without moving your lips?' Back to the Angus voice: 'I didn't want you to see my teeth, madam!' 'Are you wearing my dentures? Return them at once!'
3. Beginners get prompted line by line so the servant knows what's coming ('Call your servant in.' 'James!' 'Say in his accent: You wanted to see me, sir?'), and soon add phrases of their own as the trainer drops out.
4. Desynchronized lips: master inserts an 'err' before the servant's sentences.
5. Demonstrate, then class plays in pairs, swapping partners.

**Rules Stage Two:** Servant makes physical blind offers, seized by the throat the master must gasp 'Pay me the money you owe me!' Clutching her feet earns 'Forgive me, sir! I can't get the dog out of the lawnmower!' Control is now shared, but restrain yourself, don't swamp the master.

**Variations:** Two-way Lip-Sync: each dubs another's voice. Please don't, it was invented as an ending gag, and he's watched five or six players spend ten unintelligible minutes on it. If challenged, keep it short and insist on one-on-one. Lip-Sync from Offstage: offstage voices on microphones dub onstage bodies, starting with weeping, laughing, sighing, screaming before adding words, actors justify with blind offers, excellent for funny voices, or run it with each voice standing beside its body as a 'shadow'. With Audience Volunteers: actors provide the voices, coach volunteers to overdo the lips, voices force activity ('I'll get you a juice from the refrigerator', 'Shall I lie on the bed so you can examine me, Doctor?'), and a Judge hauls discourteous voices onstage to apologize. Variant: two players onstage with one volunteer, one player doing all three voices.

**Coaching:** Servants underdo lip movement to hide errors, make them overdo it, audiences enjoy wholehearted mistakes. If voice always leads lips or lips always lead voice, correct it: control must be shared. Scenes develop more easily this way than when both players speak.

## Invisibility

**Players:** 2 or more

**Setup:** One player is endowed with invisibility and behaves normally (the straight man), the others live in a world where invisible people do not exist. His setup: Deborah sits on the sofa reading, Tom enters.

**Goal:** React spontaneously and believably to the impossible instead of thinking intellectually.

**Rules:**
1. Endow partner or partners with invisibility, act out the consequences.
2. There are no invisible people in this world, so the seer must rationalize: insane, drugged, haunted, or tricked. An invisible hand grasping your arm feels like a stiff arm with inexplicable white indentations. An invisible collision feels like slipping or a heart attack. Sitting on an invisible sleeper gives inexplicable warmth and softness, suspension in mid-air, and the sleeper wakes paralysed.
3. Track by ear and touch: stare at the point where the voice stopped.
4. Establish familiarity early so changes stay minimal: 'Is that you Deborah? Oh, no! You're invisible again.'
5. Fear drives action: 'Don't stay if you are afraid. Run out of the door!' 'But then the scene will be over.' 'It's your house! You live here! You'd come back eventually.'

**Variations:** Each Partner Endows the Other: neither can see the other. Coach them to blunder into each other safely, gasping and screaming. Tom grabs a chair against the terror, Deborah strikes it to see what holds it up. Rule additions: don't mime objects, don't wear hats or nobody knows if the hat is invisible or floating.

**Coaching:** Prime them first: remember being alone at night when something crashed in the next room, imagine a coffee mug floating up or a book reading itself. Side-coach errors the instant they occur: 'You can hear where the voice is coming from!' 'Don't look at Deborah. Look at the book!' Beginners scream at doors opening, inappropriate. Two mutually invisible players who never touch and never leave are refusing to be altered, make them sit on each other and flee. 'Scream when she screams! The sound was right beside you!' Intellectual reactions are always too slow. Provide real doors: openable, slammable, boltable.

## The Ghost Game

**Players:** 3 or 4 skilled players

**Setup:** Characters enter a deserted interior, haunted house or old temple. His framing: travelling to a summer house, the car breaks down far from anywhere at dusk, you see a house in the trees, it seems derelict but the electricity is on, there's no phone, so you bed down for the night.

**Goal:** Escalating mutual invisibility until nobody can see anybody, ending in collective panic.

**Rules:**
1. Anyone leaving the stage plays the Invisibility Game on re-entry: first entry everything visible, every subsequent post-exit entry invisible.
2. Leaving together doesn't exempt you: anyone who leaves re-enters unable to see the others.
3. When all players are back onstage, none of them can see any of the others.
4. Endings: everyone screaming and hiding under cushions, thrashing at imaginary spirits, huddling in corners, or back at the car, now a wreck, peering in at their own dead bodies.

**His example flow:** Peggy fetches the cases from the car, re-enters blind, drops them, flees shrieking. Ron chases, returns instantly, stares around: 'She ran round to the back of the house . . . Oily?' Oily, unseen: 'I can't get this case unlocked. It's got her medication.' Ron stares straight at the case without seeing Oily: 'Augh!' Oily touches him: 'Ron?' 'Someone touched me!'

**Variations:** Suggested cast: a cub reporter, a sceptical scientist, the chairman of a Society for Paranormal Research, and a medium.

**Coaching:** Expect to explain the rules several times and still field 'But can we see each other?' Someone will leave and come back pretending to be invisible. Recommended for very occasional use, skilled players only in public.
