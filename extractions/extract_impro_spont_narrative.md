# SPONTANEITY

## 1. First Answer Drills (ll. 4113-4207)
**Players:** Leader plus one student at a time, class watching
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Expose and cure the rejection of first thoughts. "Teaching her to accept the first idea will make her seem far more inventive."
**Rules:** Fire quick prompts: "Say a word." "Tell me a colour." "Invent a name for a stone." "What's the opposite of 'starfish'?" After each answer, immediately ask "What was the first word you thought of?" and make the student say that one. His girl says "Pig" (first thought "Pea"), "Red" (first thought "Pink"), "Ground" (first thought "Pebble"). Another says "cabbage" while his lips formed an O shape: the real first answer was "Orange". On "What's the opposite of starfish?" the student gapes, Johnstone shouts "Answer, say it, I can see that he did think of it", out comes "Sunflower", and the student is amazed it was in him.
**Variations:** "Name some objects" as fast as possible. Repeating one word counts as original: "Pebble. Another pebble. A big pebble. A pebble with a hole in it."
**Coaching:** Ask immediately, first answers never reach long-term memory so later the student will deny substituting. Blocked on one word? Say it anyway. "Say whatever occurs to you. It doesn't have to be original."

## 2. Instant Emotion, Justify Afterwards ("Be Sad") (ll. 3724-3776)
**Players:** Leader and one student
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Show that behaviour comes first and the reason arrives automatically, so you need not "think up" motivation
**Rules:** "Just be sad. See what happens." Intensify: "Hold the face in a sad position, fight back the tears. Be unhappier. More. More. Now tell me why you're in this state?" Student: "My child has died." "Did you think that up?" "I just knew." Same with action: raise your arm, then supply why (strap in the Tube, waving, milking a giraffe, any reason is right).
**Variations:** Scene version: to "What are you doing here?" answer instantly without thinking, "I just came down to get the milk, Sir", "Oh Sir, don't put me in the refrigerator, Sir." Lost in a scene? Say "Oh my God! What's that?" and images jerk into the partner ("Mother!", "A secret staircase!").
**Coaching:** "Don't choose anything. Trust your mind. Take the first idea it gives you." Justify adjectives, don't avoid acting them.

## 3. Object Mime Drill (Shelf, Pocket, Box) (ll. 4147-4307)
**Players:** One at a time before the class
**Setup:** Imaginary shelf, pocket, or box
**Goal:** Feel the difference between deciding first and letting the hand choose, since audiences delight in the second
**Rules:** First meeting: "mime taking a hat off", taking something off a shelf, out of a pocket. Leader deliberately doesn't watch, then explains the two modes: put the hand out and see what it closes on, or think first and then mime. Do both to sense the difference. Then run chains, changing the object each time. Filmed example: "Put your hand into an imaginary box. What do you take out?" "A cricket ball." "Take something else out." "Another cricket ball." "Unscrew it. What's inside?" "A medallion." "What's written on it?" "'Christmas 1948'." "Put both hands in." "A box." "What's written on it?" "'Export only.'" "Open it and take something out." "A pair of rubber corsets." "Far corners of the box." "Two lobsters." "Leave them. Take out a handful of something." "Dust." "Feel about in it." "A pearl." "Taste it." "Pear drops." "Take something off a shelf." "A shoe." "What size?" "Eleven." "Reach for something behind you." "A breast..."
**Variations:** Shelf, pocket, hat, box, behind you.
**Coaching:** Continually change the "set" (category) of the questions, that is what helps him fantasise. Run object after object until students stop bothering to think first. Watch for rejected first choices: a student mimes a book but wanted the tin of sardines, so make him take the sardines again. Expect the hand to pick up horse-turds, the audience prefers that to buckets and suitcases.

## 4. Yes Openings ("Your Name Smith?") (ll. 4311-4371)
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Feel naysaying versus yeasaying. "Those who say 'Yes' are rewarded by the adventures they have."
**Rules:** Play the interrogation twice. No version: "Your name Smith?" "No." "Are you Brown, then?" "Sorry." "Well, have you seen either of them?" "I'm afraid not." Total conflict, nothing develops. Yes version: "Your name Smith?" "Yes?" "You're the one who's been mucking about with my wife then?" "Very probably." "Take that, you swine." "Augh!"
**Variations:** High-status acceptance: "Is your name Smith?" "And what if it is?" "You've been making indecent suggestions to my wife." "I don't consider them indecent!"
**Coaching:** Low-status players tend to accept, high-status tend to block, but you can play high status and still yield. Even in a tremendous argument the actors are cooperating underneath.

## 5. Karno Inkwell Test (ll. 4347-4351)
**Players:** Tester and candidate
**Setup:** Pen, empty inkwell
**Goal:** Detect who accepts offers (Fred Karno's hiring method for comics)
**Rules:** Poke the pen into the empty inkwell and pretend to flick ink at the candidate. If they mime being hit in the eye, hire them. If they look baffled and block, don't.

## 6. Working Someone (ll. 4385-4456)
**Players:** Pairs, one driver and one assistant
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Put all responsibility on one actor to calm anxious improvisers, since "his first skill lies in releasing his partner's imagination"
**Rules:** From Bill Gaskill: one actor owns the content and development, the partner merely assists and is constantly drawn in. Diver scene: "Have you got it?" "Here it is, Sir." "Well, unwrap it." "Here you are, Sir." "Well, help me put it on." "There, Sir. I think it's a good fit." "And the helmet." Ends with the assistant pumping air while the driver descends to a wreck, three tugs on the rope. Dentist scene: patient sits and opens his mouth, assistant catches on and mimes pumping the chair higher. "Having some trouble, Sir?" "Yes. It's one of these molars."
**Variations:** Any master and servant frame, interviewer and subject, customer and shopkeeper.
**Coaching:** The audience must never realise one actor is working the other, they should believe the dentist is running the scene. Don't think of getting the assistant to do things, think up ways of getting each other into trouble ("The regular dentist is on holiday, is he?" "You seem rather young." "Is it urgent?"). Anxious actors each try to carry the whole scene, assigning responsibility to one lets everyone work calmly.

## 7. "I Love You" Scenes (ll. 4458-4509)
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Produce interesting, unforced improvisation, because lovers almost always accept each other's ideas
**Rules:** Two students say "I love you" to each other and almost always accept each other's offers.
**Coaching:** Contrast with "start something" scenes where scared improvisers talk and block ("Nice day, isn't it." "Oh I don't think so."). Students don't realise when they block, stop the scene and explain, videotape replay makes it obvious. Transcribe the dialogue live and walk them through exactly what happened, then replay: the leg-amputation scene goes from dead ("You can't do that, Doctor." "Because I'm rather attached to it.") to alive once accepted ("It's the one you amputated last time, Doctor." "You mean you've got a pain in your wooden leg?"), and a collapsing chair becomes an offer ("My God! It's spreading to the furniture!").

## 8. Offer, Block, Accept (Programmed Variants) (ll. 4625-4807)
**Players:** Pairs, teacher may referee and call categories
**Setup:** None, videotape useful
**Goal:** Give insight into the forces that shape scenes and why some partners feel impossible
**Rules:** Define terms: anything an actor does is an offer. A block wipes out the partner's premise or prevents action developing, antagonism is not blocking ("What if it is, you horrible little man!" and a servant's "No, Sir" both accept). Programmes to assign: (a) A offers and accepts, B offers and blocks: plumber scene, "You got a leak somewhere?" "Three feet of water in the basement." "Basement? You ain't got a basement." (b) Both block: "I'm here for my flying lesson." "In a bathing costume?" "I always wear a bathing costume." (c) A makes dull offers, B interesting ones: "Good morning!" "Great heavens! Frank! Did they let you out? Have you escaped?" "I hid in the laundry van... I see you've had the place redecorated." (d) Alternate offer and accept and the scene generates itself: yacht club, school reunion, "Pomeroy!" "Snodgrass!" "Butterfingers."
**Variations:** Request dull offers, interesting offers, "overaccept", "accept and block".
**Coaching:** Never think up an offer, assume one has already been made. Notice your partner is shorter: "Simpkins! Didn't I forbid you ever to be taller than me?" He sweats, fan yourself. He yawns: "Late, isn't it?" When the order to block comes from the teacher, morale doesn't collapse, which proves blocking is aggression. Accepted accidents become gifts, Stanislavsky berated an actor whose chair collapsed for breaking the fiction instead of continuing.

## 9. Two Places (ll. 4813-4819)
**Players:** 2 or more
**Setup:** Bare stage
**Goal:** Comic scenes built on conflicting realities, exploiting blocking deliberately
**Rules:** One character plays, say, waiting at a bus stop while another claims the stage is his living-room. Both sustain their own place.
**Coaching:** From the Royal Court Writers' Group, circa 1959.

## 10. Presents (ll. 4821-4849)
**Players:** Pairs, called A and B
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Train overaccepting and generate warmth between players
**Rules:** A mimes giving a present, B receives it, B gives one back, and so on. Giver does not decide what the gift is: hold your hands out (about three feet apart signals something large) and let the receiver's hands close on whatever they choose. Receiver's job is to make the received thing as interesting as possible, to "overaccept": wind it up and let it walk about the floor, sit it on your arm and let it fly off after a small bird, put it on and turn into a gorilla.
**Variations:** Playing in gibberish helps.
**Coaching:** Concentrating on making your own gift interesting produces rivalry, concentrating on making the received gift interesting produces warmth. Expect strong resistance to being overwhelmed even by mimed gifts, coax the class over the hump, then "great joy and energy are released".

## 11. Blind Offers (ll. 4851-4896)
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Interact with zero intention to communicate, curing the sulks when partners "misunderstand"
**Rules:** A strikes a pose with no intention at all. B treats it as intentional and acts on it: photographs him. A says "Thank you." B stands on one leg, bends the other, A straddles the bent leg and "nails a horseshoe on it". B thanks him and lies on the ground, A mimes shovelling earth over him, B thanks him, and so on.
**Variations:** Once mastered, hold a completely different conversation while playing: "A touch of autumn in the air today, James," stretching a hand out. "Yes, it is a little brisk," peeling A's glove off. B lies on the floor. "Is the Mistress at home?" wiping his feet on B.
**Coaching:** Offer gestures that move away from the body, freeze in position until the partner reacts. Audiences laugh at every "Thank you" and watch fascinated, the effect looks telepathic.

## 12. It's Tuesday (ll. 4898-4946)
**Players:** 2
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Train overaccepting: an inconsequential remark must produce the maximum possible effect
**Rules:** A says something matter of fact: "It's Tuesday." B reacts at full intensity: "No... it can't be... It's the day predicted for my death by the old gypsy!" Turns white, clutches his throat, staggers into the audience, bangs his head, somersaults backwards and dies gasping "Feed the goldfish." A now overaccepts the goldfish remark: jealous grief, weeping on an audience member's knee, "Fifty years' supply of ants' eggs, and what did he leave to me, not a penny. I shall write to Mother." B recovers and overaccepts that: "Your mother! You mean Milly is still alive?" Plays passionate yearning until exhausted, then throws in a fresh ordinary remark ("Forgive me Jenkins, I got rather carried away") for A to detonate, perhaps a five-minute hate tirade.
**Variations:** Johnstone classifies it as a "make boring offers, and overaccept" game.
**Coaching:** "It doesn't matter how crummy the idea is, what matters is the intensity of the reaction." Three or four sentences can fill ten minutes and astonish an audience.

## 13. "Yes, But..." and "Yes, And..." (ll. 4948-4999)
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Demonstrate exactly how caution strangles spontaneity
**Rules:** A asks questions B can say Yes to, B replies "Yes, but..." and finishes however occurs. Bad way, think the reply up before speaking: "Excuse me, is that your dog?" "Yes, but I'm thinking of selling him." "Will you sell him to me?" "Yes, but he's expensive." Logical, unfunny, no one enjoys it. Good way, launch the "Yes, but..." enthusiastically the instant the question lands and complete it off the top of the head: "You took my money!" "Yes, but I've spent it." "You're a swine." "Yes, but everyone knows that."
**Variations:** Twin game "Yes, and..." is an accept-and-offer game.
**Coaching:** From Viola Spolin, Improvisation for the Theatre. Teach both ways deliberately, uptight people see precisely how cautious they normally are, and launching then improvising the completion is very funny.

## 14. Verse (ll. 5001-5064)
**Players:** 2 or more plus leader, audience may feed lines
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Force abandonment of conscious control, since improvised rhyme cannot be planned ahead
**Rules:** Leader opens in deliberately awful verse to lower the standard: "Tom and Else take your places, / A happy smile on your faces... We'll have Tom come in and propose / 'Cause Else's pregnant, I suppose..." Players improvise a scene in couplets, saying the line first and trusting luck for the rhyme. Stuck? Say "prompt" and the leader or audience shouts the next line. The verse commands the action and the teacher enforces it: "At last I've got you in my clutches: / I'll keep you here and take your crutches." Nobody mimes anything until Johnstone yells "Take the crutches!" The partner falls: "Oh, please Sir Jasper, let me go! / You must not treat a cripple so..." Back comes "I'll sacrifice you here in old Stonehenge," and again nothing happens until "Do it, it's what you said! Everyone wants to see her dead." Mid-couplet stall: "Lie down on yonder block and pray... prompt?" Audience: "I'll kill her at the break of day..."
**Coaching:** The worse the leader's model verse, the more encouraged the actors. If a player lands a genuinely witty couplet he dries up as his standard rises, so keep demanding he go wherever the verse drags him.

## 15. Say "Yes" Tape Game (ll. 5226-5249, note 4)
**Players:** 1 actor plus a tape recorder
**Setup:** Prerecorded one-sided dialogue with pauses
**Goal:** Force total acceptance, the tape cannot adapt to you
**Rules:** Record a monologue with pauses for replies. In performance, an actor who has never heard the tape improvises against it. Sample tape: a voice from the footpath reveals it is an ant demanding to be picked up, announcing surrender ("We're fed up with being stepped on, bloody great things. Dictate your terms"), introducing Willy who climbs into your ear and blows paper bags against your eardrum, huge explosion, then "Get moving. Walk. Left, right, left, right..."
**Coaching:** You accept the tape or fail totally.

## 16. Gibberish Scenes and Impromptu Operas (ll. 5004-5006)
**Players:** Groups
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Prior freedom warm-ups before attempting verse
**Rules:** Only name-checked, no rules given: play whole scenes in gibberish, or improvise scenes as operas with sung dialogue.
**Coaching:** Students arrive turned off verse by school yet respecting it, these soften them up.

# NARRATIVE SKILLS

## 17. Yes/No/Maybe Story Invention (ll. 5514-5688)
**Players:** 2, questioner and answerer
**Setup:** None, party game
**Goal:** Trick "I can't think up a story" people into composing one effortlessly, convinced it isn't theirs
**Rules:** Answerer pretends to have thought of a story but will only say "Yes", "No", or "Maybe", following a secret arbitrary rule (Johnstone's: Yes if the question ends in a vowel, No if a consonant, Maybe if it ends in Y). Questioner interrogates and a story assembles itself: people? No. Animals? No. Buildings? Yes. Insects? Yes. "Do the insects take over the world?" "Yes." They reign alone, eat everything, grow too big to fit back into the people's buildings, and destroy the world. End by revealing the questioner invented the whole thing.
**Variations:** Party version: lure someone into authoring something outrageous (a midget dentist sexually assaulting Siamese twins), let them accuse you of perversion, then triumphantly reveal they made it up. Faubion Bowers wrote the game up in Playboy.
**Coaching:** Bend the rule to protect morale: say Yes after two Nos in a row, or abandon it entirely if the questioner wilts. Watch the questioner recapitulate and link unprompted ("Do they start out as harmless?" betrays intent) and declare "Is that the end?" when the links close.

## 18. Free Associate, Then Connect (ll. 5704-5797)
**Players:** Pairs, Actor A and Actor B
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Split storytelling into two trainable skills: generating disconnected material, and reincorporating it
**Rules:** A narrates for thirty seconds supplying deliberately unconnected material. B continues for thirty seconds connecting everything. Examples: cold night, howling wolves, concert pianist, old lady shovelling snow, becomes her long-lost pianist son, wolves closing on the hall, fur sprouting. Lighthouse with a dried sea, desert tap dripping since the beginning of time, jungle hermit, joined when the hermit turns the tap on, the seas refill, the lighthouse keeper keeps his picture. Cave of bicycle parts, smoke signals, a sonic boom, resolved as children finding Daddy has built a flying-machine and pedalling it round the valley all day.
**Variations:** Solo stage: leader says "Free-associate", lets them run, then says "Connect" or "Reincorporate". Bedtime co-telling with a child: ask questions, remember shelved elements and bring them back (Mr Elephant's leftover trunk-water squirts the woodcutter).
**Coaching:** If A tries to make it "easy" for B by joining things up himself it backfires, B has nothing to do ("She's joined it all up herself"). Writers get a lifelong tool: when stuck, look back at what you shelved instead of searching forwards.

## 19. Censor Overload Tricks (ll. 5799-5818)
**Players:** Solo
**Setup:** Paper and pencil
**Goal:** Bypass the censor by distracting or overloading him
**Rules:** (a) Write a paragraph without premeditation while counting backwards aloud from a hundred. Johnstone's attempt began: "Extra. I fall through the first storey of the car park. The driver throughout the night thought the soft concrete slit his genitals thoughtfully. Nurse Grimshaw fell further..." He quit at sixty. (b) Draw with both hands at once, keeping attention equally divided rather than switching hand to hand, blank mind, draw as fast as possible, never deciding the subject.
**Coaching:** Feels like writing after concussion. Two-handed drawing regresses the mind to about age five, and curiously both hands draw with equal skill.

## 20. Lists (ll. 5826-5899)
**Players:** Solo or group
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Stop caring what comes out of your mind
**Rules:** "Name me a list of objects, but as quick as you can." Two modes: rational jumps, dog, cat, milk, saucer, spoon, fork, or the non-associative list produced at top speed: "Duck, rhomboid, platypus, elephant's egg, cactus, Johnnie Ray, clock face, East Acton..." and "Dead nun, postbox, cat-o-nine-tails, cement hopper, mouse-juice, Pope Urban the Eighth, a blob, giant opera singer, piece of lettuce, a kazoo, a vivisected clown, a lump of interstellar dust..."
**Variations:** Typed as fast as possible rather than spoken.
**Coaching:** Nonsense is manufactured by scrambling and takes time, so speed defeats censorship. Sequential lists feel thought up, nonsequential ones arrive by themselves, like hypnagogic images. Students must be coaxed, they feel bombarded by someone else's thoughts. Create an environment where no one is punished for what emerges.

## 21. Associating Images (ll. 5901-5915)
**Players:** Pairs or circle
**Setup:** None. Developed at the studio from word-association games
**Goal:** Let sudden juxtaposed images trigger unconscious narrative material
**Rules:** One person throws out an image suddenly, the partner answers with whatever image it triggers: "A lobster..." "...With a flower in its claw." "A torn photograph..." "...An empty room." "Basket of eggs..." "...Cement mixer."
**Coaching:** Content reads itself afterwards (lobster with flower as a symbol of insensitivity) but was unconscious at the moment of pairing, leave it that way.

## 22. Characters (ll. 5927-5961)
**Players:** Groups of three
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Build characters far past what any one member would produce, by genuine agreement
**Rules:** Invent a name, then agree on details: "Betty Plum." "Big breasts." "Yes. A barmaid." "Lives in a room with blue curtains." "A stuffed toy dog on the dresser..." "Which she keeps her nightdress in." "Nylon." Continue until the group knows who she lives with, her taste in music, her secret ambition, the sorrow in her life. Second example: "George Honeywell, keeps bees, smokes a pipe, married, was married, in love with the daughter of the tobacconist, wears a soft cap, he's a voyeur, likes dogs..."
**Coaching:** Real agreement, never compromise. The instant anyone disagrees, wipe the character out and start another. Groups rapidly learn to develop characters further in ways that satisfy everyone.

## 23. Automatic Reading and Automatic Writing (ll. 5963-6067)
**Players:** Leader and volunteer, solo once learned
**Setup:** Imaginary bookshelf, later blank paper
**Goal:** Prove "there is something inside them besides themselves"
**Rules:** Reading version, elicit a poem: "Mime taking a book from a shelf." "What colour is it?" "Blue." "Did you think up the colour or see it?" "See it." "Open the flyleaf, the publisher?" "Hodson." Book title, author (Alexander Pope), page number (thirty-nine), "Find me a line of verse." "So that we..." "Seeing it or inventing it?" "Seeing it." "Next word." "It's blurred." "I've given you a magnifying-glass..." Continue until verses emerge. Handle resistance: "It's in Russian" gets "There's something written in English in the margin", "too tiny to read" gets "I'm shrinking you down to the size of the book". Writing version: stare at a blank sheet, "see" a word, write it exactly where you saw it, keep going.
**Variations:** Story-questioning cousin: begin a tale aloud, "Suddenly he hears a sound and turns to see something moving in a doorway... What is the man wearing?" The answers flash in unbidden: "A suit." "Striped." "A white dog."
**Coaching:** Stop if the person grows frightened (Johnstone halted after two verses at a public lecture). Push past the point where you feel impelled to stop, filling exercise books, the gap between what you'd choose and what emerges is the lesson.

## 24. Guided Dreams (ll. 6069-6240)
**Players:** One dreamer lying down, one guide, later two dreamers together
**Setup:** Quiet room, dreamer on the floor, eyes closed, relaxed
**Goal:** Experience effortless, choiceless imagination, things seem to happen rather than be invented
**Rules:** Guide suggests a scene and steers by questions: "You're on a beach." "Is it sandy or stony?" "Did you think that up?" "No, I just knew." Tell him he's lain there a long time, then suggest movement: along the shore, past a cave, look in, wade, climb the cliffs. Sample full journey: swimming a mountain lake, catching one particular fish, trading it to three hooded figures for a stick that vanishes an oak tree, a path upward, crying on the left, a naked woman ringed by little men, rescuing her, sleeping in leaves, she steals the wand, points it, everything goes grey and wintry, and in the mist "A huge oak tree, and three hooded figures leaping about and shouting." Story complete by brilliant reincorporation, everyone rolls about laughing.
**Variations:** Two people journey together attempting the same fantasy. Advanced students may be held or cuddled by other students during the journey.
**Coaching:** Always announce elapsed time or he refuses to get up. Watch breathing, coax near threatening areas (enter the cave, swim) but never push, steer out of bad trips. If they weep, cuddle. Abreaction protocol: it's good for you, you'll feel marvellous in half an hour, it happens to everybody. If alarm escalates, open their eyes, rock them. The point is effortless imagining, not catharsis. The guide should enter something like the same trance, and phrase suggestions as questions.

## 25. Experts (ll. 6242-6331)
**Players:** 2, interviewer and expert, audience optional
**Setup:** Mock television interview
**Goal:** Practise instant unhedged invention under pressure (adapted from Vakhtangov, who had his Turandot wise men silently solve problems like "How do you make a fly the size of an elephant?")
**Rules:** Interviewer sets impossible problems, beginning sentences without knowing the ending: "We are fortunate enough to have Professor Trout... who has just returned from Africa where he has been teaching hippopotamuses to..." Anything completes it: "do handstands", "yodel". The expert has agreed to answer as part of the game and must jump in: "How exactly did you teach them?" "It was the carrot and the whip really." "But what techniques?" "Well, I demonstrated the stitches. Then I gave them sharpened telegraph poles and about a mile of barbed wire... Leather pole-holders strapped on to the forearms, or, er, in common parlance, feet... You get 'em on to rope, and then string, and finally they'll be doing crochet." "Any examples of their work?" "I'm wearing it. Every article of my clothing was knitted by the Kilimanjaro Hippo Co-operative."
**Variations:** Interviewer refers to charts imagined on the wall and demands explanations. Demonstrations: "I believe you've brought some of your soloists with you this evening," and the expert says Yes, calling in the yodelling mushrooms or miming them out of his pocket.
**Coaching:** Hedging and waffling ("the Government has assembled a large number of these hippopotamuses...") is the failure mode, the interviewer's job is to hold the expert to the problem until he stops evading. Audiences hedge in life and deeply respect a performer who answers anything. Play Verbal Chase first.

## 26. Verbal Chase (ll. 6333-6477)
**Players:** 2
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Train questioners to change the "set" every time, jerking spontaneous answers out of the victim
**Rules:** Rapid question and answer, each question jumping category unpredictably: "Imagine a box." (victim expects "What's in it?") "Who put you in there?" "My father." "What have you got in there with you?" "A toilet." "What's written on the outside of the box?" "Ladies!" Long version: "Where are you?" "Here!" "You're not. Where are you?" "In a box." "Who put you there?" "Mummy." "She's not really your mummy. Who is she?" "She's my aunt." "What's her secret plan?" "To kill me." "What with?" "A knife." "She sticks the knife where?" "In my stomach." "Cuts it open and takes out a handful of papery..." "On the boxes is written...?" "Help!" "Who wrote it?" "I did." "Who's in the box, crawling out?" "A spider." "A spider marked...?" "SIXES." "The spider does what?" "It eats me." "Inside the spider you meet?" "My father." "Holding?" "An elephant." "By the..." "Tail!"
**Variations:** Street version: "You're in a street. What street are you in?" "Main street." "What's the shop?" "A fishmonger's." "What does the fishmonger point at you?" "A pistol." "What comes out?" "Vinegar."
**Coaching:** Answering is easy, asking is hard. Speed matters, questions slightly too fast for normal thought. Ignore content entirely however embarrassing (implied homosexuality, sexual knife imagery), just keep jerking answers out. The more insane the questions, the better. Failure mode: the questioner does all the work with closed statements ("You meet a giant." "Yes."), rephrase open-ended: "A giant does what to you?" An expert questioner becomes a better improviser.

## 27. Word at a Time (Circle Story) (ll. 6479-6548, 6871-6878)
**Players:** Circle of any size, whittled to two survivors
**Setup:** Group in a circle, optionally to a beat, optionally a darkened room with curtains pulled over the huddle
**Goal:** Experience a story told by an outside force, learn to wipe your own ideas out of the mind
**Rules:** Tell a story round the circle, one word per person, as fast as possible. Anyone who blocks is thrown out until only two remain. Example fragment: "We... Are... Going... Away... To... Explore... A... Giant!" Tougher version: each speaker points to whoever must say the next word, so nobody can anticipate their turn.
**Variations:** To a beat. Eyes shut, group pressed together in the centre (at RADA they pulled curtains over the whole group and lay "like a huge pudding"), afterwards walk about and observe perceptual changes, colours brighter, spaces resized, focus sharper. Grand finale version: the entire group speaks simultaneously, "Start with 'We' and all speak at the same time," the majority swamps the minority who go wrong.
**Coaching:** Trying to control the future only ruins it: every word added plants a hoped-for next word you must abandon. The game works only when relaxed, attentive, and unbothered about being "obvious". Not recommended for German speakers, verbs locked at the end wreck it.

## 28. Word at a Time Letters (ll. 6550-6793)
**Players:** Groups of four
**Setup:** One player writes, everyone relaxes
**Goal:** Surrender of control in written form
**Rules:** Compose a letter word by word round the group, the writer records everything including blocks. Johnstone's demonstration with a blocked Eng. Lit. graduate: "89", "The Elms", her protest "I can't think of anything else. I've got a block" yields the word "Block", then "Jan/March", "Dear Henry", "I hope Mrs Exeter has been behaving well. Mum hopes that you will take off your bra... Yours sincerely, Arthur. PS I hate you."
**Variations:** Late-night marathon version: three students wrote fourteen letters over a couple of hours with beers, armour peeling letter by letter.
**Coaching:** Letters pass through four stages if uncensored: (1) cautious or nonsensical with concealed sexual references, (2) obscene and psychotic, (3) full of religious feeling, (4) vulnerability and loneliness. Work with the same group day after day. With children, never insist on leading, Johnstone's disturbed nine-year-old supplied nearly every word while Johnstone merely connected.

## 29. Acted Word-at-a-Time Stories (ll. 6809-6878)
**Players:** Pairs before an audience, optionally a participating group
**Setup:** Bare stage, optionally audience-supplied titles
**Goal:** Fully embodied improvised narrative in deep sympathy
**Rules:** Pairs stand with arms round each other, say "We" not "I", present tense, alternating single words while acting everything out. Forbid adjectives and "But", players stall with them ("We... met... a... big... huge... terrifying... angry... black... monster... but... we... escaped"). Once basic technique is mastered, forbid escape altogether: "Kill it or be killed, or make friends with it, outwit it." Remind them there isn't really a monster, so being torn apart costs nothing. If eaten: "Go on, don't stop the game." They fight their way out of the monster's interior, or continue in heaven, find God missing and take over the place, arm-wrestle him. Titles come from the audience, two combined into one: "Dracula and the Bald Lighthouse Keeper", "Rin-Tin-Tin and the Fall of the Roman Empire".
**Variations:** One partner eyes closed while the other steers him clear of furniture. Both eyes shut, the group stands round protecting them, and if the group is warm it starts feeding the story: wind noises, flapping coats for draughts, forest rustlings, dictated encounters with animals and monsters, flying the storytellers, burying them in heaps of bodies, becoming spiders crawling over their skins. Plus the all-speak-at-once group version above.
**Coaching:** Action-avoiders produce "we-go-to-the-market-where-we-buy-bread-and-now-we-walk-to-the-beach": start them inside a womb, on another planet, or hunted for murder. With eyes shut everything hallucinates real, touch becomes forest, and the storytellers know what trees and animals are there. The group effect releases extraordinary, almost sinister energy.

## 30. Ordering Game (ll. 6996-7002)
**Players:** 2 or more, rest watch
**Setup:** Bare room
**Goal:** Get actors used to commanding and obeying, exposure to an "audience" with no success or failure to worry about
**Rules:** Actor A issues flat commands, B obeys instantly: "Sit down. Stand up. Go to the wall. Yawn. Say 'I'm tired.' Look around. Walk to the door..." No narrative intended.
**Coaching:** Early studio game, later adapted into Playwriting.

## 31. Playwriting (ll. 7004-7060)
**Players:** 3, two actors plus one "playwright", teacher coaching
**Setup:** Chairs and props as needed
**Goal:** Teach narrative composition live, and investigate exactly why the would-be writer blocks
**Rules:** The playwright dictates every word and action: "Dennis, sit on the chair, and look ill. Betty, say 'Are you feeling well?' Dennis, say 'No, could you get me a glass of water.' Betty, get Dennis a glass of water. Drink it, Dennis..." If the playwright blocks he says "prompt" and anyone supplies the next line. Three rules: (1) interrupt a routine, (2) keep the action onstage, never divert to events happening elsewhere or at another time, (3) don't cancel the story. Cancelled version: illness introduced, cured by water, gone. Corrected version: "Dennis, find that the water goes right through you, and is splashing on the floor under the chair. Betty, put the glass under the chair to catch it when it runs through again. Dennis, examine yourself to try to work out what happened..." Deflection corrected: his characters peer through a hole in the floor chatting about what's underneath, teacher says get the action back, "He tells them to tear up the floorboards, and the 'block' dissolves." Routine-breaking example: washing-up scene stalls, "Break the routine," a plate is broken on purpose, quarrel follows, they reassemble the plate, find a piece missing, investigate, find the hole.
**Variations:** Offstage-diversion fix demonstrated live: boy tells girl it's four o'clock, they drift into gossip about absent others, restart, teacher shouts "Say it's time to begin," they still drift, "Get a bucket," and suddenly it's a forced-feeding scene with a funnel, her swelling up, inspired.
**Coaching:** "Make up a story" paralyses people, "describe a routine and then interrupt it" presents no problem. As a story proceeds it establishes new routines which must in turn be broken. Audiences stay interested only while action advances between the actors, otherwise theatre decays into literature.

## 32. Routine Interruption and Prearranged Surprise (ll. 6881-6994)
**Players:** Pairs
**Setup:** None
**Goal:** Generate narrative automatically, without inventing plots
**Rules:** (a) Agree a routine, washing up, mountaineering, walking through a forest, taking goodies to Grandma, then interrupt it stupidly: mountaineers find a crashed plane, or get snowed up and start eating each other. (b) Each actor privately prearranges something the partner cannot anticipate, sprung mid-scene: a couple going to bed, the husband suddenly turns boot fetishist, or the wife starts laughing hysterically, or finds she's growing feathers.
**Coaching:** "It doesn't matter how stupidly you interrupt a routine, you will be automatically creating a narrative, and people will listen." Shakespeare's proof: the clown crawls under the sheet beside Caliban, the very last sensible move, therefore the best. Update stale fairy-tale interruptions: the princess should become a frog herself, the knight should kill the virgin and deflower the dragon. Cancelling is the great talent-wrecker: a revenge-row scene felt empty until rewritten so the girl injects her ex with a syringe at the height of the row and locks herself in the bathroom.
